# Supply Skepticism: What the Evidence Actually Shows

## The Question

Supply skeptics argue that building more market-rate housing will not improve affordability for low- and moderate-income households. Some go further, claiming new construction actively harms existing residents through gentrification and displacement.

These claims are testable. As of 2026, they have been tested extensively. The evidence is overwhelming: **building more housing reduces rents, slows displacement, and benefits households across the income spectrum.** This is true at the metro level, the city level, and the neighborhood level.

This article catalogues every major supply skeptic argument and the empirical evidence that addresses it. The literature has moved far beyond theory. We now have causal studies from six countries using natural experiments, administrative microdata, and quasi-random variation. The supply skeptic position has no empirical leg to stand on.

---

## The Foundational Papers

The intellectual framework for this debate was established by Vicki Been, Ingrid Gould Ellen, and Katherine O'Regan at NYU's Furman Center:

- **Been, Ellen & O'Regan (2019).** "Supply Skepticism: Housing Supply and Affordability." *Housing Policy Debate* 29(1), 25-40. Catalogued the major supply skeptic arguments and called for better empirical research to test them. At the time, most evidence was theoretical — the causal evidence base was thin.

- **Been, Ellen & O'Regan (2025).** "Supply Skepticism Revisited." *Housing Policy Debate* 35(1), 96-113. Assessed six years of new research. Conclusion: the call was answered. We now have "strong and growing evidence that supply really does improve affordability," with causal studies addressing every major skeptic concern.

The 2019-to-2025 arc is itself the story: skeptics had plausible theoretical concerns; researchers tested them; the concerns were not supported.

## Skeptic Argument #1: "It's All Luxury Housing — It Won't Help Regular People"

### The Claim

New market-rate housing is expensive. Poor people can't afford it. Therefore it doesn't help them. Only subsidized affordable housing counts.

### Why It's Wrong

This argument treats the housing market as segmented silos where expensive units have zero relationship to cheaper ones. It ignores the most powerful mechanism by which new supply helps: **vacancy chains** (also called moving chains).

### The Evidence: Vacancy Chains Are Real and Fast

When someone moves into a new unit, they vacate their old one. Someone else moves into that. And so on. Each link in the chain opens up housing at a lower price point.

| Study | Location | Finding |
|-------|----------|---------|
| **Mast (2023)** | 12 US cities, 686 buildings | Per 100 new market-rate units, 45-70 units freed for below-median-income households and 19-39 for bottom-quintile households. Chains reached low-income neighborhoods within 2 years. ~70% of new-unit occupants came from the same metro area. |
| **Bratu, Harjunen & Saarimaa (2023)** | Helsinki, Finland | Vacancy chains from new market-rate construction reached middle- and low-income neighborhoods "even in the short run." Crucially: compared market-rate chains to social housing chains in the same neighborhoods. By the second link, both served the same income strata. Market-rate housing took one extra link to reach low-income households — but it got there. |
| **Fang, Kim & Tyndall (2025)** | Honolulu, HI (single 512-unit tower) | Vacated homes were substantially cheaper than new units and spanned diverse locations and housing types. Income-restricted units produced fewer secondary vacancies, but those vacancies were concentrated at lower price points. In one documented case: a woman moved from a low-income rental into a condo; the person who moved into her vacated unit came directly from a homeless shelter. Two links from a luxury condo to homeless shelter throughput. |

**The Helsinki comparison is nuclear.** Social housing and market-rate housing generate functionally identical vacancy chains after the first link. The only difference is cost to taxpayers: market-rate chains cost zero public dollars. This doesn't argue against social housing — it argues that market-rate construction is a free complement.

### The Oslo Exception — And Why It Proves the Rule

Magnusson, Turner & Wessel (2019) studied Oslo and found vacancy chains were "interrupted" — pent-up demand from young adults living with parents absorbed vacated units before they could filter down. This is sometimes cited by skeptics as proof chains don't work.

But think about what this actually means: there was so much unmet housing demand that even new supply couldn't create enough vacancies to filter. The answer is not "stop building" — it's "build more." The interruption is a symptom of undersupply, not evidence against supply.

### Filtering: Not Just Theoretical Anymore

- **Rosenthal (2014).** *American Economic Review* 104(2), 687-706. First empirical measurement of filtering rates using 1985-2011 panel data. Rental housing filters at 2.5% per year (real). Owner-occupied at 0.5%. For most locations, filtering is robust.

- The vacancy chain literature has superseded the old "filtering takes decades" objection. Chains operate in 2-6 years, not decades. They are not trickle-down — they are documented household-by-household moves in administrative data.

## Skeptic Argument #2: "New Construction Causes Gentrification and Displacement"

### The Claim

Building new market-rate housing in a neighborhood attracts wealthier residents, raises the neighborhood's profile, drives up rents for existing units, and displaces long-term lower-income residents.

### Why It's Wrong

This confuses correlation with causation. Developers build where rents are already rising. New buildings are a *symptom* of neighborhood change, not the cause. And the causal evidence consistently shows new supply **reduces** displacement pressure.

### The Evidence: Supply Reduces Displacement

| Study | Location | Method | Finding |
|-------|----------|--------|---------|
| **Pennington (2021)** | San Francisco | Natural experiment: used building fires as random variation in where new housing gets built. Fires are random relative to housing demand. | Within 100m of new construction: rents fell 2%, displacement risk for current renters fell 17%. |
| **Asquith, Mast & Reed (2023)** | 11 US cities | Large new apartment buildings in low-income areas, using microdata on rents and migration. | New buildings decreased nearby rents by 5-7% relative to slightly farther locations. Increased in-migration from low-income areas. Supply effect overwhelmed any amenity effect. |
| **Li (2022)** | New York City | Microdata on new high-rises and surrounding rents/sales. | Every 10% increase in housing stock within 500ft reduced rents by 1%. Amenity effect existed but was dominated by supply effect. Net effect: rents fell. |
| **Ding, Hwang & Divringi (2016)** | Philadelphia | Credit bureau data tracking individual mobility in gentrifying vs. non-gentrifying neighborhoods. | Less-advantaged residents were *no more likely to move* from gentrifying neighborhoods than from non-gentrifying ones. |
| **Rollet (2025)** | New York City | Parcel-level building and zoning panel, quasi-experimental zoning reforms. | 10% increase in floorspace reduces rents by 4.2%. Effect continues declining for 10+ years after completion. Affordability benefits "largely accrue to households outside the rezoned neighborhoods" via migration spillovers. |

**The Pennington paper deserves special emphasis.** Building fires are as close to random as you get in urban economics. There is no plausible story where fires target neighborhoods based on housing demand. When new housing went up on fire sites, nearby rents fell and displacement dropped. This is about as clean a causal result as social science produces.

### The Amenity Effect Concern

Skeptics argue new buildings create an "amenity effect" — better restaurants, nicer streets, wealthier neighbors — that raises surrounding rents more than the supply effect lowers them.

Every causal study that has tested this finds the supply effect dominates:
- Pennington: net -2% rents within 100m
- Asquith/Mast/Reed: net -5 to -7% nearby rents
- Li: net negative despite measurable amenity effects
- Rollet: net -4.2% per 10% floorspace increase

The amenity effect is real but small. The supply effect is real and larger. Net effect: rents fall.

## Skeptic Argument #3: "Induced Demand — Building Just Attracts More People"

### The Claim

Like highways inducing more driving, new housing induces in-migration of wealthy people, negating any affordability benefit. Build it and they will come.

### Why It's Wrong

The highway analogy fails fundamentally. Highways are non-excludable (anyone can drive on them) and congestion returns to baseline as induced traffic fills capacity. Housing is excludable (each unit houses one household) and each occupied unit permanently removes one household from competing for existing stock.

### The Evidence: Most Movers Are Already Local

| Source | Finding |
|--------|---------|
| **Mast (2023)** | ~70% of households moving into new market-rate buildings came from the same metro area. |
| **Phillips (2026), Census microdata analysis** | 60-70% of people in recently-built housing came from the same metro area. This share is not meaningfully different from people in older housing. New housing does not attract a disproportionate share of out-of-area movers. |

People don't browse Redfin looking for new apartments in random cities. They get a job offer, a college acceptance, want to be near family — then look for housing in that place. New housing captures demand that already exists. It does not create it.

The relevant counterfactual: if you don't build new housing, those 60-70% of local movers compete for existing units instead, bidding up rents for everyone. The "induced demand" concern has the causality exactly backwards.

## Skeptic Argument #4: "Land Is Special — Supply and Demand Don't Apply"

### The Claim

Land is finite. Housing isn't like other goods. You can't just "make more." Therefore standard economics doesn't apply.

### Why It's Wrong

Land is finite in two dimensions. Zoning artificially constrains the third dimension (height). Most cities are nowhere near their physical capacity — they're at their *regulatory* capacity.

### The Evidence: Relaxing Constraints Works

| Study | Location | Finding |
|-------|----------|---------|
| **Giacoletti, Korevaar & Qian (2025)** | Netherlands (national policy) | Mid-1990s policy relaxed residential land supply constraints near major cities. New supply attracted high-income individuals, reduced house prices in nearby high-income neighborhoods, and increased local employment. Land was centrally allocated (not market-selected), eliminating endogeneity. |
| **Gu & Munro (2025)** | Minneapolis | After 2040 Plan eliminated single-family zoning: home prices 16-34% lower, rents 17-34% lower than synthetic control. Even before construction boom materialized — expectations of future supply reduced speculation. |
| **Soltas & Gruber (2026)** | Los Angeles | Permitting alone explains one-third of the gap between home prices and construction costs. Developers pay 50% premium ($48/sqft) for pre-approved land. Median permit takes 2 years — 40% of total site-acquisition-to-occupancy time. The land "scarcity" is largely regulatory delay. |

**The Soltas/Gruber paper reframes the entire debate.** Much of what looks like "land scarcity" is actually *permitting scarcity*. When you can pay someone to pre-clear the regulatory process, developers pay a 50% land premium for the privilege. The constraint isn't physical — it's bureaucratic.

## Skeptic Argument #5: "Neighborhood Effects Are Harmful Even If Citywide Effects Are Positive"

### The Claim

Maybe more supply helps at the metro level, but the specific neighborhood where it's built gets worse off. New buildings bring congestion, parking pressure, shadows, and character change that harm existing residents.

### Why It's Wrong

This was the most credible skeptic concern in 2019, when neighborhood-level causal evidence was scarce. It is now the most thoroughly debunked.

### The Evidence: Neighborhood Effects Are Positive

Every causal study measuring neighborhood-level effects finds net rent reductions:

| Study | Scale | Rent Effect |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| Pennington (2021) | 100m radius | -2% |
| Asquith/Mast/Reed (2023) | Nearby units | -5 to -7% |
| Li (2022) | 500ft buffer | -1% per 10% stock increase |
| Rollet (2025) | Neighborhood | -4.2% per 10% floorspace increase |
| **Mense (2025)** | Local rent distribution | -0.4 to -0.7% per 1 new unit per 100 existing rental listings |

**The Mense (2025) paper is the most powerful single result.** Published in *Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics*, it used weather-driven construction delays as an instrument (weather is random relative to demand). Finding: new housing supply shifts the **entire rent distribution** downward — not just luxury rents, all rents. This kills the "only helps rich people" argument at the neighborhood level with a top-tier journal publication.

## Skeptic Argument #6: "We Should Only Build Affordable Housing"

### The Claim

Public resources and scarce land should be devoted exclusively to income-restricted affordable housing, not market-rate development.

### Why It's Wrong

This is an aspiration, not a policy. The math doesn't work.

### The Math

In the United States, approximately 300,000-400,000 subsidized affordable units are built annually. The national housing shortage is estimated at 3-7 million units (depending on methodology). At current subsidy levels per unit ($300,000-$500,000 for LIHTC), closing the gap through subsidized housing alone would cost $1-3.5 trillion. Annual federal spending on all affordable housing programs combined is roughly $50 billion.

To close the gap with subsidized housing alone would require 20-70 years at current funding levels, assuming zero additional households form and zero existing affordable units deteriorate. Both assumptions are false.

Meanwhile, the Helsinki evidence shows that market-rate vacancy chains produce functionally identical outcomes to social housing chains by the second link — at zero public cost.

**The correct framing is not market-rate vs. affordable. It is market-rate AND affordable.** Supply skepticism that blocks market-rate housing doesn't redirect resources to affordable housing — it just reduces total supply while leaving the affordable housing budget unchanged.

### What Happens When You Don't Build Market-Rate

Without new supply, wealthier households with more resources outbid lower-income households for existing housing stock. This is the filtering mechanism *in reverse*: wealthier people filter *down* into housing that would otherwise serve lower-income families.

This is not theoretical. It is the documented mechanism driving displacement in supply-constrained cities like San Francisco, New York, and Los Angeles.

---

## The Reverse Counterfactual: What Happens When You *Don't* Build

Supply skeptics focus entirely on potential harms of building. They never model the alternative.

When cities restrict new housing:
- Existing residents compete for a fixed stock, bidding up rents
- Wealthier households outbid poorer ones for the same units
- Landlords can raise rents without fear of vacancy (no competitive pressure)
- Displacement increases as rents rise faster than incomes
- Homelessness increases as the bottom of the market tightens

The question is never "build vs. don't build." It is "build vs. the consequences of not building." Every city that has tried to solve affordability through restriction has gotten worse. No exceptions.

## For Advocacy Use: The Five Strongest Single Facts

When you need to convince someone in 60 seconds:

1. **"Per 100 new market-rate units, 45-70 units open up for below-median-income households within 2 years."** (Mast 2023, Journal of Urban Economics)

2. **"Within 100 meters of new construction, rents fell 2% and displacement risk dropped 17%."** (Pennington 2021, natural experiment using building fires in SF)

3. **"New market-rate housing and social housing produce identical vacancy chains by the second link."** (Bratu et al. 2023, Helsinki administrative data, Journal of Urban Economics)

4. **"New housing supply shifts the entire rent distribution downward — not just luxury rents, all rents."** (Mense 2025, Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics)

5. **"70% of people moving into new apartments already lived in the same metro area."** (Mast 2023; confirmed by Census microdata analysis)
