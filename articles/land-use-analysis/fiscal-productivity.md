# Fiscal Productivity & Land Use Analysis

## Overview

Framework for evaluating land-use policy by what it physically produces and what it costs — not by political framing. Combines the academic evidence base on density and fiscal productivity with practical methods for analyzing municipal fiscal health.

---

## Step 1. Ignore the Framing

Land-use debate is fought on vibes: "neighborhood character," "community input," "responsible growth." These are marketing terms. Skip them.

Instead ask:
- What does this policy **physically produce** on the ground? (units, lane-miles, pipe-feet, service calls)
- What does it **cost** to build and maintain? Over what lifecycle?
- What **revenue** does it generate per acre?
- **Who pays** and **who benefits**?

---

## Step 2. Go to the Evidence

### Evidence Hierarchy

1. **Peer-reviewed meta-analyses** — Ahlfeldt & Pietrostefani 2017: 102 studies, 202 elasticity estimates
2. **Peer-reviewed empirical studies with large samples** — Goodman 2019: 30-year US county panel; Carruthers & Ulfarsson 2003: 283 metro counties
3. **Government-commissioned research with panel review** — TCRP Report 74, Halifax Growth Scenarios
4. **Practitioner fiscal analyses with transparent math** — revenue-per-acre calculations
5. **Think-tank reports from ideologically committed orgs** — treat as advocacy, verify independently

**Don't false-balance.** The academic literature on sprawl costs, induced demand, and fiscal productivity of density is as one-sided as climate science. Presenting refuted contrarian work as "the other side" is misinformation.

---

## What the Fiscal Literature Shows

- Compact development generates 10-100x more revenue per acre than sprawl
- Suburban infrastructure costs more to serve per acre than it generates in revenue
- Urban cores cross-subsidize suburban areas, not the other way around
- New development looks profitable for 20-30 years; the replacement cycle at 30-50 years reveals the true cost
- The direction is not in dispute; the magnitude varies by study
- The U-shaped density-cost curve is real but the ascending right limb kicks in at densities no US city outside Manhattan reaches

---

## Revenue-Per-Acre Method

The core analytical tool. Compare tax revenue generated per acre against infrastructure and service costs per acre.

### Inputs
- **Property tax revenue per parcel** — from county assessor rolls (Prop 13 basis in CA)
- **Sales tax revenue** — where applicable by land use
- **Parcel acreage** — from GIS/assessor data
- **Infrastructure serving the parcel** — linear feet of road, water, sewer, storm drain
- **Maintenance cost per linear foot** — from city CIP and public works budgets
- **Replacement cost** — infrastructure lifecycle: roads 20-30 yrs, water/sewer 50-80 yrs
- **Service calls** — police, fire, EMS per acre by land use type

### The Calculation
```
Revenue productivity = (annual tax revenue) / (parcel acres)
Cost burden = (annual maintenance + amortized replacement) / (acres served)
Net fiscal position = Revenue productivity - Cost burden
```

### What It Reveals
- A downtown mixed-use parcel generating $200K/acre in tax revenue on 0.25 acres of road frontage is fiscally productive
- A cul-de-sac SFR generating $3K/acre requiring 200 linear feet of exclusive road, water, and sewer is a net drain
- The SFR looks solvent for 25 years because the road is new; when the road needs replacement ($300-500/linear foot), the math collapses

## Infrastructure Lifecycle — The Ponzi Model

Strong Towns framework: suburban development is a growth Ponzi scheme.

**Cycle:**
1. Build new subdivision → developer pays for initial infrastructure
2. Property tax revenue covers maintenance for 20-30 years (looks solvent)
3. Infrastructure reaches replacement age → replacement cost exceeds accumulated revenue
4. City builds NEW subdivision to generate revenue to fund replacement of OLD subdivision
5. Now two replacement obligations exist, funded by building a third subdivision
6. Eventually: obligations exceed capacity to grow → deferred maintenance → service collapse

**Key metric:** Infrastructure replacement cost vs. total property tax revenue from the area it serves. If replacement cost > 20 years of tax revenue, the development pattern is structurally insolvent.

---

## Political Structure of the Subsidy

- In district-based representation, suburban districts often outnumber urban ones
- Beneficiaries of the cross-subsidy hold the votes to maintain it
- "Neighborhood character" protection = revealed preference for subsidized infrastructure
- State preemption (SB 9, SB 79, Builder's Remedy) exists because local political capture makes reform from within structurally impossible

---

## Evaluating Political Actions Against Stated Goals

Take the stated goal, compare to the actual action, check against evidence.

1. What did they say they want?
2. What did they actually vote for / implement / cut?
3. Does the action advance the stated goal according to the evidence?
4. If not, who actually benefits?

---

## Rebuttal Patterns

**"People chose suburbs, so suburbs are optimal"** — Revealed preference argument ignores massive policy distortions: highway subsidies, exclusionary zoning, parking mandates, FHA patterns. People chose from a distorted menu.

**"Density is expensive to serve"** — True only above ~24,000 people/sq mi (Manhattan). For all practical US policy decisions, moving toward density reduces per-capita costs.

**"We can't afford transit/complete streets"** — You can't afford not to. Car-dependent infrastructure is the fiscal drain.

**"This reflects community input"** — Community input in land-use is structurally captured by incumbent property owners. Renters, future residents, and people priced out don't testify.
