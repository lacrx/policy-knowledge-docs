# Japan Housing Model — National Zoning, Depreciation, and Affordability Through Supply

## Overview

Japan is the only major developed economy where housing has become more affordable over the past 30 years. Tokyo — the world's largest metro (37M people) — has a rent-to-income ratio of ~22%, lower than most comparable global cities. Japan builds ~800,000-900,000 housing units per year, roughly double the US per capita rate, despite declining population.

This is not an accident of demographics. Japan's affordability preceded its population decline and is driven by three policy mechanisms: a national zoning code that legalizes housing nearly everywhere, a depreciation regime that treats housing as a consumable rather than an investment vehicle, and a construction sector that delivers units at 30-50% below US costs.

The model produces excellent supply-side outcomes but has significant gaps in tenant protections, social housing, and support for vulnerable populations. It is the purest real-world example of YIMBY theory: legalize, build, let filtering work. Where it succeeds and fails illuminates what supply alone can and cannot do.

## Pillar 1: National Zoning and Ministerial Permitting

### 13 Zones, Set Nationally

Japan's City Planning Act establishes 13 use zones nationwide, administered by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). Municipalities apply these zones but cannot invent new categories or add restrictions beyond the national code.

The zones are **inclusive/nuisance-based**: each zone specifies the *maximum nuisance* allowed, and everything below that threshold is permitted. Housing is allowed in 12 of 13 zones (all except Industrial Exclusive). There is no distinction between single-family and multifamily — a fourplex and a mansion have equal legal standing in residential zones.

| Zone | Housing? | Character |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Category 1 Low-Rise Residential | Yes | Quietest. 2-3 story limit. Still allows multifamily. |
| Category 2 Low-Rise Residential | Yes | Adds small shops, restaurants |
| Category 1 Mid-Rise Residential | Yes | Hospitals, universities allowed |
| Category 2 Mid-Rise Residential | Yes | Adds larger retail |
| Category 1 Residential | Yes | Most mixed. Some offices, hotels |
| Category 2 Residential | Yes | Adds karaoke, pachinko |
| Quasi-Residential | Yes | Commercial emphasis, housing allowed |
| Neighborhood Commercial | Yes | Small-scale retail + housing |
| Commercial | Yes | Dense mixed-use |
| Quasi-Industrial | Yes | Light industry + housing |
| Industrial | Yes | Factories + housing |
| Industrial Exclusive | **No** | Only zone excluding housing |
| Urbanization Control | Limited | Agricultural preservation, restricted development |

Contrast: US cities routinely have hundreds of zone types. San Francisco has 89. New York's zoning resolution runs 3,780 pages. Over 1,600 municipalities in New York State alone can adopt independent land-use laws. Japan has 13 categories, set once, at the national level.

### Building Confirmation (Kenchiku Kakunin)

Japan's permit process is called **building confirmation** — the term itself is telling. It is a confirmation of compliance, not a discretionary approval.

- **Ministerial**: if a project conforms to the Building Standards Act, the permit **must** be issued. No public hearing. No design review committee. No neighbor veto. No "community character" finding.
- **Private review bodies**: since 1998 reforms, applications can go to government building officials or private confirmation agencies. Competition between reviewers accelerated processing.
- **Timeline**: pre-2025, simple wooden buildings reviewed in **7 days**. Post-April 2025 code revision, statutory period up to **35 days** (4-go exemption eliminated, structural review scope expanded). Compare US: plan check itself may take 2-6 weeks, but discretionary entitlement in California can add 6 months to 5+ years.
- **No CEQA equivalent**: Japan has environmental impact assessment for large-scale developments, but nothing comparable to California's CEQA, where individual housing projects face environmental review lawsuits. The ministerial/discretionary distinction — the same distinction that matters for CEQA applicability — is resolved entirely in favor of ministerial.

### Anti-NIMBY Structure

The national government controls zoning. PM Koizumi's 2002 Urban Renaissance Policy made it easier to rezone land and sped up permits. NIMBY objections have no institutional pathway to block conforming development. The central government is structurally pro-housing — a feature, not a byproduct.

Tokyo built **145,000 homes** in 2018 — more than all of California (population 3x larger).

## Pillar 2: Housing as Depreciating Consumable

### The Depreciation Regime

Japanese residential buildings depreciate rapidly by policy, culture, and financial structure. This is the single most consequential difference from Anglo housing systems.

**Key data**: Japan housing depreciation rate: **~6% per year**. US housing depreciation rate: **~1% per year** (Yoshida, Penn State/UCLA). A 6x differential that fundamentally changes housing's role in the economy.

**Statutory useful life** (for tax depreciation):
- Wooden residential: **22 years**
- Steel-frame: **34 years**
- Reinforced concrete: **47 years**

These are tax code numbers, not physical reality — wooden houses actually survive ~65 years on average. But the tax code creates self-fulfilling prophecy through five reinforcing mechanisms:

1. **Bank financing**: Mortgage valuations based on remaining statutory life. A 25-year-old wooden house has near-zero collateral value. Banks won't lend against it.
2. **Tax incentives**: Accelerated depreciation means buyers who could claim deductions prefer new construction.
3. **Building code ratchet**: Major seismic code revisions (especially 1981) create value cliffs. Pre-1981 stock is perceived as unsafe. 76% of Japan's 53.6M housing units were built after 1981.
4. **Cultural norm**: "A house that has been lived in by someone else" carries stigma. Only **14.7%** of housing transactions are existing homes (vs. ~90% in the US/UK).
5. **Scrap-and-build industry**: demolition + rebuild is routine, efficient, and cheap enough to be the default upgrade path.

### Anti-Speculation Effects

Housing depreciation structurally eliminates housing as an investment vehicle:

- "Investment or portfolio growth is effectively impossible" — homes lose half their value in 10 years and become "almost entirely worthless" in 25.
- No bidding wars. No blind offers. No renovation-for-resale culture.
- Homebuyers optimize for **personal use** (custom layouts, preferences) rather than resale value. Depreciation paradoxically creates design freedom.
- Households save more in financial instruments rather than treating home equity as retirement savings. US homeowner net worth is ~40x renter net worth — a gap driven largely by home appreciation. Japan's gap is far smaller.
- Housing depreciation equates to **4% of GDP** annually — value that simply vanishes. This is the cost of treating housing as infrastructure rather than investment.

**Land vs. structures**: Land and buildings are treated as entirely separate assets. Structures depreciate; land retains and often gains value. Tokyo core ward land appreciated 10-14% annually in 2024-2025. When buying older properties, buyers effectively purchase land and pay demolition costs to remove the worthless structure. Any speculative impulse channels into land, not the built environment.

### Shifting Slowly

The scrap-and-build model is beginning to change:
- Government campaigns promote renovation (especially post-earthquake retrofitting)
- Existing home transaction share rising slowly
- Environmental cost awareness growing
- But structural incentives (tax code, bank lending, building codes) still heavily favor new construction

## Pillar 3: Construction Cost and Industrial Policy

### Costs 30-50% Below US

Japanese housing construction costs are dramatically lower than US equivalents:

- Wooden residential: **0.8-1.2 million yen/tsubo** (~$155-230/sq ft)
- Reinforced concrete: **1.4-1.8+ million yen/tsubo** (~$270-345/sq ft)
- US comparison: median new construction cost $150-400/sq ft depending on region, with California at the high end

Cost advantages come from: streamlined permitting (no discretionary review overhead), standardized national building code (no patchwork of local requirements), efficient demolition/rebuild cycle, and competitive private review bodies.

### Factory-Built Housing at Scale

Japan has the world's most mature prefabricated housing industry:

- **12.6%** of new housing starts are factory-built (2023). ~103,400 prefab units.
- Over **10 million** prefab homes built since the 1960s.
- Market estimated at **$16.4 billion** (2024).

**Major manufacturers** (house makers):

| Manufacturer | Notable |
|---|---|
| Sekisui House | 2.6M+ homes since 1960. ~15,000 detached/year. 93% zero-energy-home rate. |
| Daiwa House | Founded 1955. Massive international conglomerate. |
| Sekisui Heim | Modular units assembled on-site in a single day after factory production. |
| Toyota Home | Auto manufacturing expertise applied to housing. 40-year warranty. |
| Panasonic Homes | Electronics integration, flexible floor plans. |
| Sumitomo Forestry | Premium wood construction. 9 US factories (2024), expanding to 15+. |

**Production data**: Sekisui House's Shizuoka factory produces ~20 houses per day. Standard warranties: 20 years (Toyota: 40 years). ISO 9001 quality management across industry.

**Key insight**: Japanese prefab does NOT compete on price. Costs are ~8% higher than site-built. It competes on **speed, quality, seismic performance, and consistency**. The industry pivoted from cost-reduction in the 1970s-80s toward quality after public rejection of cheap mass-produced housing.

**Customization**: manufacturers claim "100% customizable" within modular systems based on tatami measurements (90 x 180 cm). Practical result described as "20 shades of beige" — genuine dimensional flexibility within a narrow aesthetic range.

### Japanese Builders' US Expansion

Japan's demographic decline is pushing house makers into the US market:
- Sekisui House acquired MDC Holdings for **$4.9B** (2024), now 5th-largest US homebuilder
- Daiwa House acquired Stanley Martin ($560M) and CastleRock ($750M)
- Sumitomo Forestry agreed to acquire Tri Pointe Homes for **$4.5B** (2025)
- Total Japanese capital deployed: **$7-8 billion** over seven years
- Driver: Japan domestic margins compressed to mid-single digits; US margins 10-18%

## Outcomes: What Works

### Affordability Through Volume

- **Tokyo rent-to-income ratio**: ~22% (compare: NYC ~30%, London ~35%, San Francisco ~35%+)
- Tokyo housing stock has **tripled** in 50 years
- **Filtering works mechanically**: rapid depreciation pushes 10-year-old units to well below new construction prices. The oldest stock becomes genuinely affordable. Unlike US cities where 1960s apartments are "vintage" and expensive, Japanese units from the same era are cheap because they're approaching zero structural value.
- Price stability: no sustained housing price boom since the 1989 bubble collapse. The system produces housing as infrastructure, not as a speculative asset class.

### Homelessness

Japan has dramatically lower homelessness than any comparable economy:
- **Official count**: 2,820 (2024), down from 25,296 (2003). Rate: 0.2 per 10,000.
- **US comparison**: 771,480 (2024). Rate: 17.5 per 10,000.
- Even generous estimates adding hidden homelessness (internet cafes, 24-hour McDonald's, manga cafes — estimated 100,000-300,000 people) put Japan at roughly **4x lower** than the US per capita.
- This is both undercounting AND genuinely less. Japan undercounts (narrow "rough sleeper" definition, cultural pressure to remain invisible). But the gap is too large to be explained by methodology alone.
- Affordable housing stock from depreciation/filtering provides a genuine floor. When the oldest housing is cheap, fewer people end up with no housing at all.

### Vacancy as Feature, Not Bug

- National vacancy rate: **13.8%** (2023). ~9 million akiya (empty houses).
- But vacancy is overwhelmingly **rural**, not urban. Rural depopulation leaves houses in towns losing residents. Tokyo vacancy: ~11%, which is healthy market friction.
- Akiya are not proof of overbuilding in cities — they're proof that Japan builds where people live and doesn't try to prevent the decline of places people leave.

## Outcomes: The Gaps

### Tenant Protections

Japan's rental market has significant equity gaps:

- **60.4% discrimination** against "atypical" tenants: foreigners, elderly, disabled, single mothers, welfare recipients, LGBTQ+ individuals face systematic landlord discrimination.
- No anti-discrimination housing law comparable to the US Fair Housing Act. Landlord discretion is broad and largely unchecked.
- No rent stabilization. Rents are market-set. The system relies on supply abundance to keep rents affordable rather than regulation.
- Tenant protections are the weakest pillar — a structural gap, not an oversight.

### Social Housing

- Only **7.1%** of housing stock is public/social. Compare: Vienna ~43%, Netherlands ~30%, UK ~17%.
- Public Housing Act (1951) stock is aging and shrinking. Danchi (public housing complexes) from the 1950s-70s are being demolished without replacement.
- No equivalent to Vienna's GBVs, no limited-profit housing associations, no equity lock-in institutions.
- The system relies almost entirely on private market production + filtering. There is no institutional backstop.

### Hidden Homelessness

- Official count (2,820) uses a narrow "rough sleeper" definition — people sleeping outdoors in a designated area on the count night.
- Internet cafe refugees (net cafe nanmin): estimated 4,000+ in Tokyo alone.
- Broader estimates of housing-insecure populations: 100,000-300,000 nationally.
- Cultural pressure to remain invisible (shame around visible poverty) suppresses both help-seeking and official counts.
- Even with generous adjustment, Japan's rate is still dramatically lower than the US — but the official number understates the problem significantly.

### Depreciation Externalities

- **4% of GDP** in annual housing value destruction — an enormous economic cost.
- Environmental waste from scrap-and-build cycle. Demolition + reconstruction generates far more construction waste than renovation.
- Embodied carbon in constant rebuilding. Environmental costs unaccounted in the economic model.
- Psychological effect: homeownership doesn't build intergenerational wealth. The "American Dream" wealth-building pathway doesn't exist.

## Five-Pillar Scoring

Using the complete-housing-position framework:

| Pillar | Japan's Score | Assessment |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| P1: Legalize | **A+** | Best in world. National zoning, ministerial permitting, no neighbor veto, 13 inclusive zones. |
| P2: Institutional Capacity | **F** | 7.1% social housing, shrinking. No GBVs, no equity lock-in, no institutional nonprofit developers. |
| P3: Dedicated Revenue | **D** | No dedicated housing revenue stream. No LVT. GHLC (now JHF) provided subsidized mortgages, not production funding. |
| P4: Tenant Protections | **D** | No anti-discrimination law, no rent stabilization, 60.4% discrimination rate. Market abundance is the only protection. |
| P5: Construction Costs | **A** | 30-50% below US. 12.6% factory-built. National code eliminates regulatory patchwork. Efficient demolition/rebuild. |

**Overall**: Japan proves what supply-side policy alone can achieve — and where it hits a ceiling. Affordable on average, but with no equity floor for the most vulnerable.

## Transferability Assessment

### What transfers directly

- **National zoning with inclusive zones**: the single most transferable element. State preemption of local zoning (CA SB 35, SB 79 model) moves in this direction. The principle — housing legal everywhere, ministerial permitting for conforming projects — requires no cultural prerequisites.
- **Ministerial permitting**: eliminate discretionary review for conforming projects. Already the mechanism behind California's SB 35 streamlined approvals.
- **Pre-approved designs / pattern books**: catalogs of permitted designs that skip design review. Directly applicable, already attempted in scattered US jurisdictions.
- **Factory-built housing normalization**: end zoning discrimination against manufactured/modular housing. Japanese house makers already entering US market with $7-8B in acquisitions.

### What transfers with modification

- **Split-rate LVT as anti-speculation mechanism**: Japan uses depreciation to prevent housing speculation. LVT achieves the same anti-speculation effect (penalize unproductive land holding) without the 4% GDP cost of destroying housing value annually. LVT is the economically superior version of what depreciation does accidentally. See: land-value-tax.
- **Standardized national building code**: the US has IBC/IRC but with extensive local amendments. Federal preemption of local code amendments would capture much of Japan's regulatory efficiency.

### What does not transfer

- **Housing depreciation regime**: dependent on tax code, bank lending practices, seismic code ratchet, and deep cultural norms. Cannot be legislated into existence. Also, the 4% GDP cost and environmental waste argue against wanting to transfer it.
- **Cultural attitudes toward new vs. used housing**: 85% new-construction market share reflects cultural preferences built over generations.
- **Homogeneity advantage**: Japan's social cohesion (lower income inequality, strong social safety net outside housing, cultural norms against visible poverty) reduces the demand-side pressure on housing that US inequality generates.

## The Honest Synthesis

Japan is the proof-of-concept for YIMBY Pillar 1. No other country demonstrates as clearly that legalizing housing + building abundantly + ministerial permitting = affordability. Tokyo is the world's largest city and one of its most affordable — not because of demographics (affordability preceded population decline) but because of policy.

But Japan also demonstrates Pillar 1's ceiling. Without institutional capacity (Pillar 2), dedicated revenue (Pillar 3), or tenant protections (Pillar 4), you get:
- Affordable on average but 60.4% discrimination against vulnerable tenants
- No permanent affordability — just market-rate filtering that works until it doesn't
- No counter-cyclical stability — the bubble era proved what pure market housing does under speculative pressure
- 4% of GDP in annual wealth destruction as the price of anti-speculation

The complete position: **build like Tokyo** (Pillar 1 + 5), **channel like Vienna** (Pillar 2 + 3), **protect like California** (Pillar 4). Use LVT instead of depreciation to get the anti-speculation benefits without the 4% GDP waste.
