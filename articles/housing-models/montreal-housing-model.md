# Montreal Housing Model — Plexes, Soft Rent Control, and Accidental Affordability

## Overview

Montreal is the largest affordable major city in North America. As of 2025, a 2-bedroom rents for ~$1,930 (asking) vs. Toronto's $2,690 and Vancouver's $3,170. Existing-tenant rents are even lower — ~$1,200 for a purpose-built 2-bedroom, protected by soft rent control.

Montreal's affordability is not the product of a single policy but of multiple reinforcing factors: a housing stock dominated by small multifamily plexes (78% of residences), soft rent control without vacancy decontrol, high supply elasticity, a renter-majority political culture (63.6% renters), and — crucially — a French-language requirement that limits immigration-driven demand relative to English-speaking Canadian cities.

The model is eroding. Asking rents rose 71% from 2019 to 2025. Financialization of rental stock is accelerating. The provincial government cancelled AccèsLogis (Quebec's main social housing program) in 2023 with no adequate replacement. Montreal is losing its affordability edge in real time, but remains significantly cheaper than comparators.

The transferable lessons are about what NOT to do: don't exclude multifamily from residential land, don't impose costly development fees on small buildings, don't allow vacancy decontrol. The non-transferable parts are the century of plex building and the language barrier.

## The Plex Stock: Why Montreal Is Different

**78% of Montreal residences are four-unit-or-fewer plexes** (2016 Census). Two out of three Montrealers live in apartments in buildings with fewer than five storeys. The "Montreal plex" — stacked dwelling units with curved exterior staircases — is a form unique to Quebec, built continuously since the 19th century.

This is NOT "missing middle" housing. In Montreal, it IS the dominant housing form. The term "missing middle" describes what other cities lost; Montreal never lost it.

### Why Plexes Survived

- Montreal is one of the few chartered Canadian cities with municipal bylaw powers that can override provincial/federal building codes. This allowed continued plex construction despite restrictive national codes.
- Montreal historically did not exclude multifamily from residential land — no American-style single-family-exclusive zoning.
- No costly development fees on small buildings.
- No regulatory preference for expensive housing or sprawl over compact infill.

### Supply Elasticity

Montreal has much higher supply elasticity than Toronto or Vancouver. When demand increases, Montreal responds by building more. Toronto and Vancouver respond with price increases.

**Housing starts (Montreal CMA)**:

| Year | Starts | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| 2021 | ~32,000 | 30-year record |
| 2022 | ~24,000 | -25% |
| 2023 | 15,235 | -37% (nationwide construction crunch) |

2024 composition: 80% rental apartments, 10% single-family, 10% condos. Greater Montreal captured **28.2%** of Canada's new rental inventory vs. Toronto at 14.4% and Vancouver at 5.4%.

## Soft Rent Control: The TAL System

### How It Works

Quebec operates **soft rent control** through the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL). Unlike hard caps, the TAL publishes annual guidelines based on a formula incorporating municipal taxes, insurance, energy costs, maintenance, and capital expenditures. Landlords propose increases; tenants can accept, refuse (while staying), or leave.

If a tenant refuses, the landlord has one month to apply to TAL for arbitration. If they don't apply, the lease renews without modification. Less than 1% of rental stock goes to arbitration annually.

### Recent Guidelines

| Year | Recommended Increase | Notes |
|------|---------------------|-------|
| 2024 | ~4% | |
| 2025 | 5.9% (unheated) | Highest in 30+ years |
| 2026 | 3.1% baseline | New formula: 3-year CPI average |

### No Vacancy Decontrol (Critical Distinction)

When a tenant moves out, the landlord must disclose the lowest rent paid in the prior 12 months on the mandatory lease form (Section G). New tenants can challenge excessive increases at TAL.

Ontario, by contrast, allows landlords to reset rent to any price between tenants. Result: Ontario turnover units saw **35.6% average rent increases** (40.4% in Toronto) in 2022-2023.

This single policy difference — vacancy decontrol vs. no vacancy decontrol — is one of the largest drivers of the Montreal-Toronto rent gap.

### Enforcement Gap

Despite the disclosure requirement, actual rent increases (7.2% in 2025) far exceed TAL guidelines. Asking rents for new tenants rose 71% from 2019 to 2025. There is no mandatory rent registry, making enforcement of between-tenant controls difficult in practice.

### Bill 31 (2024)

Mixed bag:
- **Strengthened**: renoviction protections (presumed refusal for non-response, compensation up to 24 months' rent)
- **Weakened**: eliminated lease transfer rights (landlords can now reject transfers without reason), closing tenants' main tool for preserving below-market rents

## Why Montreal Stayed Cheap: The Full Stack

Seven reinforcing factors, not one single cause:

1. **Plex housing stock**: lower construction costs per unit than towers. 78% small multifamily.
2. **Zoning permissiveness**: never excluded multifamily from residential land. No expensive development charges historically.
3. **Supply elasticity**: market responds to demand with construction, not just price increases.
4. **Soft rent control without vacancy decontrol**: protects existing tenants, constrains between-tenant increases.
5. **Renter political culture**: 63.6% of Montreal dwellings rented. Median voter is a renter. Political demand is for affordability, not property value appreciation.
6. **Language barrier**: Quebec's French-language requirements limit immigration demand relative to English-speaking cities. The "single most important non-financial factor."
7. **Lower incomes/higher taxes**: Quebec incomes lower and income taxes highest in Canada, creating a more price-conscious market.

## Social and Cooperative Housing

### Scale

- **Montreal non-market total**: 67,885 units (~7.7% of housing stock)
- **OMHM (public housing)**: 20,810 units in 918 buildings, housing 55,000 Montrealers
- **Cooperatives**: ~1,200 coops province-wide, ~22,500 units, 50,000+ people
- **Quebec total social housing**: 74,000 units

### HLM (Public Housing)

Rent fixed at 25% of household income. Waiting list: 12,424 households (April 2026), average wait 5 years. One-third of units need renovations. Maintenance deficit: **$1.079 billion** (2026), up 25% from 2023.

### AccèsLogis Cancellation (2023)

The provincial CAQ government cancelled AccèsLogis, Quebec's main social housing financing program since 1997. Replaced with PHAQ, which requires only 10-year affordability sunset (vs. permanent). No new HLM construction under the replacement. Over 30,000 Quebecers on waiting lists.

### SHDM

Paramunicipal nonprofit managing 5,217 affordable units at 76% of CMHC median market rent.

### Failed Inclusionary Zoning (20-20-20)

Adopted 2021, required 5+ dwelling projects to include social/affordable/family units. After 3 years, only one development complied — developers consistently paid penalties instead. Replaced in 2025 with a single 20% obligation for large projects only.

## The Gaps: Is Montreal Losing Its Edge?

### Rent Escalation
71% increase in asking rents since 2019. Gap between existing-tenant rents (~$1,200 for 2BR) and asking rents (~$2,050) shows new tenants bear the affordability burden.

### Enforcement Failures
No mandatory rent registry despite disclosure requirements. Bill 31 added penalties but enforcement depends on individual tenant challenges.

### Financialization
Large institutional investors entering Montreal's rental market, drawn by lower acquisition costs than Toronto/Vancouver. Drives up rents and displaces long-term tenants.

### Social Housing Decay
53% of Montreal's social housing had D or E condition grades in 2026. $3.6B allocated through 2028 for repairs.

### Airbnb
Montreal implemented strict regulations: seasonal operation (June 10-Sept 10 only), principal residence requirement, banned in 6+ unit buildings, complete bans in some boroughs. Fines up to $10,000/$100,000. ~8,900 listings, majority estimated illegal. Triggered partly by a deadly 2023 fire in an illegal Old Montreal Airbnb that killed 7 people.

## Five-Pillar Scoring

| Pillar | Montreal | Assessment |
|--------|----------|------------|
| P1: Legalize | **A-** | Historically permissive — never excluded multifamily. No SFH-exclusive zoning. But development fees rising, failed inclusionary zoning, some recent restrictions. |
| P2: Institutional Capacity | **C+** | 7.7% social housing, cooperative sector, OMHM. But AccèsLogis cancelled, maintenance deficit growing, no new HLM construction. |
| P3: Dedicated Revenue | **D** | No dedicated housing revenue stream. AccèsLogis was program-funded, now cancelled. No LVT, no payroll levy. |
| P4: Tenant Protections | **B+** | Soft rent control, no vacancy decontrol, renoviction protections, Airbnb restrictions. But enforcement weak, lease transfers eliminated. |
| P5: Construction Costs | **B** | Plex form is inherently cheap. Low historical development fees. But rising costs, new regulations adding friction. |

**Overall**: Montreal's affordability is more accident of history than replicable policy design. The plex stock took a century to build. The language barrier is non-replicable. The transferable policies (no vacancy decontrol, permissive zoning for small multifamily, soft rent control) are individually adoptable but don't recreate the full stack.

## Transferability

### Replicable

- **No vacancy decontrol**: pure policy choice. Ontario's 1998 vacancy decontrol was political, not structural.
- **Soft rent control with independent tribunal**: TAL's guideline-based system maintains investment incentives while protecting tenants. The C.D. Howe Institute found rental starts per capita in Quebec exceeded Ontario's over two decades — rent control didn't kill supply because the balance was right.
- **Legalize missing middle by-right**: don't exclude multifamily from residential land. Minneapolis and Oregon moved in this direction.
- **Mandatory standardized lease with rent disclosure**: simple policy tool.
- **Aggressive short-term rental regulation**: seasonal restrictions, principal-residence requirements.
- **Supply elasticity via streamlined approvals**: not requiring expensive discretionary processes for moderate-density infill.

### Non-Replicable

- **78% plex housing stock**: took a century to build. Cities dominated by SFH can't retroactively create this.
- **Charter city code override**: Montreal's ability to override national building codes enabled continued plex construction.
- **French-language demand limiter**: moderates immigration-driven demand without discriminatory policy.
- **Renter supermajority**: 63.6% renters means median voter is a renter. In 30-40% renter cities, homeowner interests dominate.
