# Finland Housing Model — Housing First, ARA System, and Public Land Banking

## Overview

Finland is the only EU country where homelessness declined continuously for over a decade (2008-2023). Long-term homelessness dropped 72%, from 3,600 to ~1,000. This was not accidental — it was the result of a deliberate national policy: Housing First, which provides permanent housing without preconditions (no sobriety, no treatment compliance, no "readiness" assessment).

But Housing First is the headline, not the whole story. Finland's broader housing system includes: ARA/Varke-funded social housing (~11% of stock, ~404,000 homes), Helsinki's extraordinary 70% municipal land ownership enabling public land banking at scale, cost-price rent setting, and a construction sector that delivered 40,000+ dwellings annually at peak.

The 2024-2025 reversal is equally instructive. After austerity cuts halved housing advice budgets while demand surged 80%, homelessness rose 20% in a single year — the largest increase ever recorded. The model works, and it breaks when you stop funding it.

## Housing First

### The Principle

Permanent housing provided **without preconditions**. No sobriety requirement. No treatment compliance. No "readiness" assessment. Housing is a human right and a prerequisite for recovery, not a reward for it.

Before 2008, Finland used the "staircase" model common across Europe: homeless individuals progress through rehabilitation steps before receiving permanent housing. That model failed to reduce long-term homelessness.

### Y-Foundation (Y-Säätiö)

Founded 1985. Finland's largest Housing First operator:
- **19,000+ homes** across ~60 locations nationwide
- Rents ~20-40% below private market rates
- Receives discounted state-backed loans to purchase properties
- Stakeholders include Finnish Red Cross, Church Council, Association of Finnish Local/Regional Authorities
- In 2016, co-established the Housing First Europe Hub with FEANTSA

### Shelter-to-Apartment Conversions

The most striking transformation — Helsinki converted nearly its entire shelter system to permanent housing:

| Metric | 1985 | 2016 |
|--------|------|------|
| Shelter/hostel beds | 2,121 | 52 |
| Supported housing units | 127 | 1,309 |
| Independent rental apartments (formerly homeless) | 65 | 2,433 |

By 2019, Helsinki had only one permanent service center for emergency accommodation with 52 beds. 4,600 homes provided in the first ten years.

### Cost Savings

Annual savings per person housed vs. shelter/emergency model:
- Conservative: **EUR 9,600-15,000**/person/year
- Broader (including reduced evictions, healthcare): **EUR 15,000-52,000**/person/year
- Hospital days dropped 80%, arrests dropped 75% in Pitäjänmäki unit
- Tampere: EUR 250,000 saved annually
- 80% housing retention rate

Total program investment 2008-2019: **EUR 270+ million** (~$293M), split between central government and municipalities. PAAVO I (2008-2011) alone: EUR 201.1M.

### The Results

| Year | Single Homeless | Long-term Homeless |
|------|----------------|-------------------|
| 1987 | ~18,000-20,000 | — |
| 2008 | 7,960 | 3,600 (Housing First baseline) |
| 2015 | — | 2,400 (-33%) |
| 2021 | 4,396 | 1,318 |
| 2023 | 3,429 | 1,018 (low point) |
| 2024 | 3,806 | 1,010 (+377 overall, first increase since 2012) |
| 2025 | 4,579 | 1,306 (+20% YoY, largest recorded rise) |

From 1987 peak to 2023 trough: **over 80% reduction**. Long-term homelessness 2008-2023: **72% reduction**.

### The 2024-2025 Reversal

After 11+ consecutive years of decline, homelessness began rising. Drivers:
- Housing allowance and social assistance cuts
- Housing advice service budgets **halved** while demand surged **80%**
- Wellbeing services counties still recovering from health/social services reform
- Rising rents, construction costs, unemployment
- New demographic: low-income individuals losing housing due to minor income fluctuations — the system "reacting too late"

**The lesson**: Housing First works, and it breaks when you defund it. The model requires sustained political commitment across government changes — Finland sustained it through four governments over 15 years before austerity reversed the gains.

## ARA System (now Varke)

### Structure

ARA (Housing Finance and Development Centre of Finland) administered state housing policy until March 2025, when it was replaced by **Varke** (Centre for State-Subsidised Housing Construction). The change was administrative — functions continue.

### How It Works

- Grants subsidies, support, and guarantees for housing construction/purchase/renovation
- **Interest subsidy loans** from commercial banks or MuniFin (municipally-owned), state-guaranteed
- Loan period: up to **41 years** for long-term loans
- Since 2008, ARA guarantees extensively used to source low-cost private-loan finance via MuniFin
- Detailed financial sustainability assessments before project approval

### Scale

- **~404,000 state-subsidized homes** (~11% of all housing stock)
- ~60% regular rental apartments, ~26% homes for special groups, remainder right-of-occupancy
- Annual production: 7,000-9,000 apartments at stable levels
- Helsinki: social rental housing ~**20%** of stock
- One-third of all Finnish apartments built with some form of state subsidy

### Rent Setting

Rents determined by **cost-price principle** — covering construction debt service + maintenance + reserves. Not market pricing. Functionally identical to Vienna's Kostenmiete.

Tenant selection based on need: housing urgency, wealth, and income. Priority given to homeless and those in urgent need with lowest incomes.

### Recent Changes (2025)

- Income limits for state-subsidized housing reintroduced
- Right-of-occupancy housing production ended
- ARA's housing fund to be merged into state budget in 2026

## Finnish Housing Structure

### Tenure

- Homeownership: **~69.2%** (2023, declining)
- Renters: 36% of households (up from 30% in 2010). Exceeded 1 million households for first time in 2023.
- ~44% live in detached houses, ~38% in apartment buildings

### Housing Companies (Asunto-osakeyhtiö)

Finland's dominant ownership form. When you buy a Finnish apartment, you buy **shares in a limited liability company** that owns the building. The company owns the property; shareholding confers occupancy rights. Similar to cooperatives but organized as limited companies. Traditional housing cooperatives are extremely rare.

### Right-of-Occupancy (Asumisoikeus)

Introduced 1990, modeled on Sweden's system. Resident pays 15% of purchase price for permanent tenancy. Combines rental flexibility with ownership permanence. Government ended production of new units in 2025 — a gap without a clear replacement.

### Helsinki HITAS System

Price-controlled owner-occupied apartments built on city-owned land. Maximum prices set by real production costs, regulated for at least 30 years. Units allocated by lottery. Helsinki is phasing out HITAS — criticized for effectively subsidizing well-off purchasers. 700 apartments found owned by people with multiple HITAS units.

## Public Land Banking: Helsinki's 70%

Helsinki owns approximately **70% of its land area** — one of the highest rates of municipal land ownership in Europe. This gives the city extraordinary control over housing development.

### How It Works

- 60-70% of annually granted building rights are on city-provided plots
- Land is **leased, not sold**, with extensive controls and restrictions
- Planners draw specifications; developers must build to those specs
- Quality competitions for development rights (similar to Vienna's Wohnfonds Wien)

### MAL Agreement Targets

Under the state-municipal MAL (land use, housing, transport) agreement:
- Helsinki target: at least **8,000 new dwellings/year** (from 2023)
- **25%** must be subsidized/regulated rental (aim: 35%)
- 30% unsubsidized but with regulated ownership
- 90% in priority zones (high public transport accessibility)

### Construction Rates

| Year | Finland-wide Starts |
|------|-------------------|
| 2018 | 44,000+ (peak) |
| 2017-2021 | 40,000+/year |
| 2023-2024 | <20,000 (decade low) |

2023-2024 collapse driven by rising interest rates, construction costs, and tighter lending. State-supported production rose to ~40% of total as private construction collapsed — public investment as counter-cyclical stabilizer.

## Five-Pillar Scoring

| Pillar | Finland | Assessment |
|--------|---------|------------|
| P1: Legalize | **B** | Helsinki densifying actively. 90% of new housing in transit-priority zones. But no Japanese-style national zoning reform; local control remains. |
| P2: Institutional Capacity | **A-** | ARA/Varke, Y-Foundation, municipal housing companies. 11% social housing. But right-of-occupancy ended, HITAS ending, recent austerity weakening institutions. |
| P3: Dedicated Revenue | **B+** | State-backed interest subsidy loans via MuniFin. ARA housing fund (being merged into budget 2026 — a risk). Helsinki's land leasing generates revenue. No LVT or payroll levy. |
| P4: Tenant Protections | **B+** | Housing First (strongest homelessness response globally). Cost-price rents in social housing. But reversing under austerity. |
| P5: Construction Costs | **B-** | 40,000+/year at peak. But construction collapsed to <20,000 in 2023-2024. No factory-built housing sector comparable to Japan. |

**Overall**: Finland demonstrates what committed institutional housing policy can achieve — and how quickly gains reverse under austerity. Housing First is the proof of concept for unconditional housing; ARA is a functional (if smaller-scale) analog to Vienna's system; Helsinki's land ownership is a structural advantage no other major Western city possesses.

## Transferability

### Replicable

- **Housing First principle**: permanent housing without preconditions, with optional wraparound support. Already adopted in Canada, France, Denmark, and parts of the US. 80-88% housing retention rates in major studies.
- **Shelter-to-apartment conversion**: every city has shelters that could be converted.
- **Cost-based rent setting**: identical to Vienna's Kostenmiete. Universally applicable.
- **Data-driven homelessness tracking**: annual municipal surveys covering all 297 municipalities.
- **State-backed housing finance**: MuniFin model of municipally-backed low-cost lending.

### Partially Replicable

- **Public land banking**: Helsinki's 70% ownership is nearly unique, but any city can begin acquiring land. Vienna's Wohnfonds Wien demonstrates smaller-scale public land banking.
- **MAL agreements**: state-municipal housing production targets with social housing quotas. Require political will, not unique conditions.

### Non-Replicable

- **Small, homogeneous population** (5.6M): easier consensus and coordination.
- **High institutional trust**: Finns trust government to manage housing.
- **Strong welfare state baseline**: universal healthcare and income support prevent purely income-driven homelessness.
- **Political culture of consensus**: programs ran through four governments over 15 years.

### Housing First Elsewhere — Mixed Results

| Place | Results |
|---|---|
| Medicine Hat, Canada | First to achieve "functional zero" chronic homelessness (2021). Rose to 34 by 2025 due to affordable housing shortage. |
| Canada (At Home/Chez Soi) | Largest Housing First study (CAD $110M). Housed 85% of time vs. 60% treatment-as-usual. |
| United States | 80-88% retention. But national homelessness continues rising — Housing First addresses individual chronic homelessness, not systemic shortage. |
| France | Scaled nationally following successful pilots. |

**Critical insight**: Housing First cannot compensate for fundamentally inadequate housing supply. Canada's homelessness rose 20% nationally (2018-2022) despite widespread Housing First implementation. The model works for the people it reaches; it does not solve the supply problem that generates homelessness at scale.
