# Oceanside Density Cap: Cross-Verified Findings

Source: October 2026 PRA release from California Coastal Commission. ~1,800 documents across 8 releases. Response to CPRA #9 filed by Thomas LaCroix. Covers CCC-City of Oceanside correspondence on density cap, inclusionary housing, Coast Highway, and related state law matters.

Cross-verified against: `policy-knowledge-docs` KB articles (ca-housing-enforcement, policy-impact-filing-analysis, supply-skepticism-rebuttal), etrakit discretionary filing data (2020-2026), HCD APR Table A (Oceanside reference, 2018-2025).

## Executive Finding

The 86 du/acre density cap (LCP-6-OCN-25-0029-2) is a downzoning that killed Oceanside's market-rate downtown housing pipeline. Three-check filing analysis confirms the causal link. CCC's suggested modifications, while framed as fixes, created a structural mechanism for the Coastal Act to override state density bonus law on coastal resource grounds. The correspondence reveals a city with strained administrative capacity, chronic LCP compliance gaps, and a planning department that lost institutional knowledge through turnover.

## Filing Impact Analysis (Three-Check Method)

Per `policy-impact-filing-analysis` KB methodology. All data from etrakit discretionary filings (D2/DB/RD project types, housing only) and HCD APR Table A.

### Check 1: Prior Years, Same Geography (Downtown District)

Downtown housing filings were robust and accelerating before the cap.

| Year | Etrakit Filings | Notable Projects |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| 2020 | 6 | Small infill |
| 2021 | 7 | Modera Neptune (360u), 901 Pier View (64u) |
| 2022 | 11 | 810 Mission (206u), Modera Neptune, 901 Pier View |
| 2023 | 14 | Regal Cinema (332u), 712 Seagaze (179u), 801 Mission (230u), Rodeway Inn (271u) |

APR confirms: 2022 downtown proposed units = 1,111. 2023 = 1,465. Peak filing years.

Council voted the 86 du/acre cap in October 2023. SB 330 vesting burst followed immediately: Nov-Dec 2023 saw five filings with SB 330 preliminary applications locking in pre-cap density. Developers anticipated the freeze and raced to vest.

### Check 2: Same Year, Different Geography (Non-Downtown)

Non-downtown housing filings held steady at 8-9/year through 2024-2025. APR non-downtown units: 1,185 in 2024, 1,919 in 2025 (driven by large non-downtown projects). Citywide pipeline works. Development did not stop. It stopped in the cap zone.

### Check 3: Same Year, Same Geography (Post-Cap Downtown)

| Year | Downtown Filings | Character |
|------|-----------------|-----------|
| 2024 | 10 | Refiled/revised pre-cap projects using SB 330 vesting. No genuinely NEW large market-rate projects. |
| 2025 | 11 | Only 50+ unit project is 100% affordable (3296 Mission, 100u). Market-rate filings: 2-unit condo addition, condos, triplex. |
| 2026 | 0 housing | One medical office building. Zero residential filings year-to-date. |

APR confirms: 2025 downtown proposed = 35 units total. Largest market-rate project: 24 units. No density bonus projects. For comparison, 2023 downtown proposed = 1,465 units.

### Three-Check Conclusion

All three checks pass cleanly. The density cap killed large market-rate downtown development. Confirming indicators from the KB methodology:

1. **SB 330 vesting burst**: Developers filed SB 330 preliminary applications immediately after council vote (Nov-Dec 2023), confirming they anticipated infeasibility under the cap.
2. **Affordable-only large filings**: The only 50+ unit downtown project in 2025 is 100% affordable (which can use DBL to exceed 86 du/acre). Per KB: "If the only new filings are 100% affordable projects, this confirms the cap is the binding constraint on market-rate development."
3. **Small-project-only market-rate**: Market-rate filings shrank to projects small enough to fit under 86 du/acre (2-4 unit infill, condos).
4. **Duration**: Zero large market-rate downtown filings for 24+ months post-cap (Oct 2023 to present). The longer the freeze, the stronger the evidence.

## Obstruction Pattern Analysis

Cross-referenced against municipal obstruction patterns from `ca-housing-enforcement` KB.

### Coastal Act Shield (SM#3)

KB pattern: "Route denials through CCC to override density bonus and state mandates."

SM#3 creates exactly this mechanism. While framed as adding density bonus provisions to the Downtown District (filling a genuine gap), the operational effect is:

- Coastal Act savings clause: DBL "shall not supersede" the Coastal Act
- City "may deny" DBL waivers/concessions if "significant adverse coastal resource impacts"
- Minor LCP inconsistencies from DBL "may be approved" only if no significant coastal resource impacts
- Required analysis of coastal resource impacts for each DBL project

Before SM#3, the Downtown District had no DBL provisions at all, and the City applied state DBL directly. The gap was real. But the "fix" routes every density bonus application through a Coastal Act review that can override state housing mandates. The New Commune v. Redondo Beach case (2024) opened this door; SM#3 builds the doorframe.

This matters for SB 79 (effective July 2026). Oceanside Transit Center qualifies as Tier 1 (120 du/acre, 75ft adjacent). If the City uses SM#3's Coastal Act savings clause to deny density bonus projects that exceed 86 du/acre base density on coastal resource grounds, the cap is effectively preserved despite state preemption.

### SB 330 Violation (5,500 Unit Cap)

KB: "No downzoning below Jan 1, 2018 levels" (Gov. Code 66300).

CCC identified the 5,500 dwelling unit maximum in the Downtown District Redevelopment Plan as an SB 330 violation. SM#1-2 converted it to a non-binding "target." This means:

1. The political basis for the density cap (protect the 5,500 cap) was never legally enforceable.
2. The "target" conversion is functionally meaningless. A non-binding target has zero regulatory effect. The pipeline already approaches 5,141 units (2,300 existing + 2,841 approved/under review). The "target" will be exceeded by projects already in the pipeline, before the cap expires in 2029.

### Traffic Monitoring Gap (Preserved Pretext)

CCC's 2021 certification of unlimited density required a Traffic Monitoring and Mitigation Plan. As of 2025-2026 correspondence, the City had not conducted monitoring because "no major downtown developments have finalized construction."

This is both a compliance gap and a preserved pretext. The City cannot claim traffic impacts justify the density cap when it never measured traffic impacts. But the unfulfilled monitoring obligation provides future ammunition: if traffic worsens as the pipeline delivers, the City can argue monitoring data supports maintaining or tightening the cap at the 2029 expiration.

### Downzoning Framed as Reasonable

CCC accepted 86 du/acre as reasonable (2x other SD County coastal cities). But the relevant comparison is not peer cities today. It is Oceanside's own prior zoning:

- **Jan 1, 2018 baseline** (SB 330 reference): 43 du/acre
- **Feb 2022**: Unlimited density (CCC-certified with modifications)
- **Oct 2023**: 86 du/acre cap adopted by council
- **Feb 2026**: 86 du/acre certified by CCC

The 86 du/acre is 2x the 2018 baseline. It is not downzoning relative to 2018 under strict SB 330 reading. But it is a massive downzoning from the unlimited density that was the certified LCP from 2022-2026. Projects that were feasible at 200-500 du/acre with density bonus are infeasible at 86 du/acre base (even with DBL, max ~172 du/acre, and now subject to Coastal Act override via SM#3).

CCC coordinated with HCD to confirm no SB 330 violation. HCD apparently agreed, using the 2018 baseline (43 du/acre) rather than the 2022 certified density (unlimited). This reading may be correct on the statute. It does not change the market reality: the filing data shows the cap killed the pipeline.

## Administrative Dysfunction

Recurring theme across correspondence. Not a peripheral finding; this is the mechanism through which compliance gaps persist and the LCP stays outdated, which in turn preserves CCC gatekeeping authority.

Direct quotes from City staff:
- "Unfortunately the folks who may have worked on that project are no longer with the City"
- "We don't have the staffing resources to keep up with every change in state law"
- "It appears that this may have been an oversight since the project planner left the City shortly after taking the item through the ZA process"
- Principal Planner working on a side project to determine "whether there are other items that may have fallen through the cracks"

Concrete consequences:
1. Downtown excluded from density bonus provisions. Nobody knows why. Lost to staff turnover.
2. Uncertified ordinance used as baseline for inclusionary LCPA. Staff who prepared it didn't know it was uncertified.
3. Traffic monitoring never implemented (4 years after certification requirement).
4. CCC filings delayed months due to City staffing constraints.

The pattern is directionally consistent: every gap in LCP maintenance is an invitation for CCC to add conditions when the City is eventually forced to submit an amendment. Not updating the LCP doesn't save resources; it forecloses the development revenue that would fund them. Self-fulfilling resource scarcity.

## State Law Implications

### AB 2097 (Parking Preemption)

Attorney Christopher Pederson notified CCC (April 2023) that AB 2097 prohibits minimum parking requirements within 1/2 mile of major transit stops. Both Oceanside Transit Center and Coast Highway Sprinter station qualify. CCC Senior Attorney Robin Mayer acknowledged awareness. Coast Highway Incentive District parking requirements are preempted by state law.

### SB 79 (Transit Density, Effective July 2026)

Oceanside Transit Center qualifies as Tier 1 per `ca-housing-enforcement` KB: COASTER + Amtrak + Sprinter likely exceed 72 trains/day threshold. This mandates 120 du/acre and 75ft height adjacent to station. The 86 du/acre cap is below SB 79 Tier 1 density for parcels within SB 79 walking distance.

Key question: does SM#3's Coastal Act savings clause allow the City to maintain 86 du/acre for coastal parcels within the SB 79 zone? The tension between Coastal Act and state housing mandates is unresolved (New Commune opened the door but didn't close it). This will be the next legal battleground.

### Density Bonus Law

The supply-skepticism-rebuttal KB documents that market-rate housing generates vacancy chains reaching low-income households within 2-6 years (Mast 2023, Bratu et al. 2023). Every market-rate unit blocked by the density cap is a vacancy chain that doesn't happen. The cap doesn't just block luxury towers; it blocks the filtering mechanism that produces affordable housing through the market.

The etrakit data confirms: DBL projects were the primary vehicle for large downtown development (Modera Neptune 360u, Regal 332u, 901 Mission 273u, Rodeway Inn 271u). All used density bonus to exceed 43 du/acre. Under the 86 du/acre cap with SM#3's Coastal Act savings clause, new DBL projects face: (1) lower base density ceiling, (2) coastal resource review on every waiver/concession, (3) potential denial on coastal grounds. The incentive structure now favors either small projects under cap or 100% affordable projects that bypass market constraints.

## What CCC Got Right

CCC staff pushed back on several anti-housing provisions:
- Converted illegal 5,500 unit cap to non-binding target (SB 330 compliance)
- Required density bonus provisions for Downtown District (filling a real gap)
- Negotiated inclusionary threshold from 10 down to 7 units (closer to peer cities)
- Required uncertified ordinance language to be properly incorporated
- Added CDP requirement for affordable housing agreement recording

## What CCC Missed or Enabled

- Accepted 86 du/acre without analyzing filing impact. The data showing the cap killed market-rate downtown development was available from etrakit.
- SM#3's Coastal Act savings clause creates a permanent mechanism to override state housing mandates in the coastal zone, extending beyond this particular LCPA.
- Did not address the traffic monitoring compliance gap as a prerequisite for the density cap.
- Did not require the City to demonstrate that the density cap is consistent with RHNA obligations (even though no RHNA sites are downtown, the pipeline was contributing to citywide housing production).

## Fiscal Productivity and Equity

### Self-Fulfilling Resource Scarcity

City Planner Sergio Madera: "We don't have the staffing resources to keep up with every change in state law." This frames state housing law as an unfunded mandate. It isn't. The projects enabled by that state law (DBL, streamlining, ADU law) generate the property tax, impact fees, and utility revenue that fund the planning department. Not incorporating state law into the LCP doesn't save resources. It forecloses the revenue that would fund them.

Pattern across the correspondence: the City doesn't update the LCP, which preserves gaps, which gives CCC modification authority when forced to address them, which adds Coastal Act conditions, which kills projects, which reduces revenue, which means fewer staff, which means the LCP doesn't get updated. Self-fulfilling.

Each gap individually looks like understaffing. Together they all point the same direction: toward preserving local discretion and CCC gatekeeping. Whether conscious strategy or an institution that learned "not doing things" protects the status quo, the effect is identical. "We don't have resources" is unfalsifiable cover either way.

Per the fiscal-productivity KB: dense mixed-use infill generates orders of magnitude more revenue per acre than low-density development, at lower marginal infrastructure cost. Every downtown project killed by the density cap is lost property tax base, lost impact fees, lost utility connections. The City chose less revenue, then claims insufficient resources to administer the laws that would generate more revenue.

### Inclusionary Arithmetic

City raised the affordable set-aside from 10% to 15% to "produce more affordable housing." The density cap killed the projects that trigger inclusionary requirements. 15% of zero is zero. The only inclusionary units being produced are from pre-cap vested projects. Once those build out, the mechanism is dead in downtown until the cap lifts or a 100% affordable developer files.

Pre-cap math: a 300-unit market-rate DBL project at 15% inclusionary produces 45 affordable units directly. Per Mast (2023) vacancy chain ratios, the same 300 units free 135-210 additional units for below-median-income households within 2 years. Total affordable housing impact: 180-255 units served per project, at zero public cost.

Post-cap: the same site produces nothing. No market-rate project files. No inclusionary units. No vacancy chains. The residents who would have moved into freed-up units stay in overcrowded, cost-burdened housing.

The people paying for "community character" are the people who can't afford to live there.

## SB 79 Collision (Forward-Looking)

SB 79 (Gov. Code 65912.150 et seq.) took effect July 1, 2026. Per ca-housing-enforcement KB:

**Oceanside Transit Center classification:** COASTER commuter rail + Amtrak Pacific Surfliner + Sprinter light rail converge at this station. If combined train counts exceed 72/day across both directions (likely, given COASTER alone runs 40+ and Amtrak adds 12+), the station qualifies as **Tier 1**: 120 du/acre minimum density, 75ft height, adjacent to station.

**The conflict:** The 86 du/acre density cap is below SB 79 Tier 1 density for parcels within SB 79 walking distance of the transit center. Those parcels are in the Downtown District. The cap and the mandate are directly contradictory.

**SM#3 as potential SB 79 override:** The Coastal Act savings clause in SM#3 states DBL "shall not supersede" the Coastal Act. If the City argues that Coastal Act authority also supersedes SB 79 transit density mandates, the 86 du/acre cap is effectively preserved for coastal parcels within the SB 79 zone despite state preemption. The legal question: does SB 79 contain its own Coastal Act exemption or not? SB 79 does not explicitly address Coastal Act tension. New Commune v. Redondo Beach (2024) found partial Coastal Act shield for Builder's Remedy but didn't resolve the broader question.

**HAA exposure (after Jan 1, 2027):** Per ca-housing-enforcement KB, SB 79 creates a presumption of HAA violation for denials in high-resource areas beginning January 1, 2027. If the City denies a project that meets SB 79 density on Coastal Act grounds, the HAA presumption shifts the burden of proof to the City. Combined exposure: HAA penalties ($10,000/unit minimum, 5x bad faith multiplier) plus mandatory attorney's fees.

**This is the next legal battleground for Oceanside downtown housing.** The density cap expires April 15, 2029, but SB 79 applies now. The 3-year overlap creates immediate litigation risk for any denial of a qualifying project near the transit center.

## Comparable City Context (from correspondence)

- **Carlsbad**: Precedent for converting growth caps to targets (SB 330 compliance). Also referenced for density bonus LCP update approach (LCP-6-CAR-20-0078-2).
- **Morro Bay**: Model for Coastal Act savings clause language in density bonus provisions (LCP-3-MRB-22-0059-2). This is the template for SM#3.
- **Newport Beach**: Density bonus LCPA also under CCC review during same period.
- **Del Mar Seaside Ridge**: Builder's remedy project with DOJ involvement. Correspondence shows CCC tracking builder's remedy applications across SD County.
