# Tax Policy and Housing Production

## Overview

Housing advocates evaluate tax policy on two axes: **who bears the burden** (progressive vs regressive) and **does the instrument incentivize or discourage housing production**. A tax can be progressive and anti-housing (high transfer taxes), regressive and housing-neutral (utility taxes), or regressive and actively harmful (impact fees that raise per-unit costs).

This article classifies every major local and state tax type on both axes, documents what housing organizations actually support, and provides a framework for evaluating ballot measures. It complements the [land-value-tax](land-value-tax.md) article (the theoretically ideal instrument) and the [fiscal-productivity](../land-use-analysis/fiscal-productivity.md) article (the analytical method for evaluating what land use patterns actually cost and produce).

## Tax Type Classification

### Regressive (burden falls harder on lower incomes)

**Sales tax.** The most clearly regressive major tax. ITEP data for California: the lowest-income households pay 7%+ of income in sales/excise taxes; top earners pay 0.8%. SPUR found Bay Area households under $30K pay 5.5% of income in sales tax vs 1.5% for the wealthiest. Renters are hit especially hard because they spend a higher share of income on taxable goods and have no asset appreciation to offset it.

California's base sales tax is 7.25%. Local layers (county, city, district) push the combined rate to 10.25-10.75% in most urban areas. Some cities exceed 11%. Each new local sales tax measure stacks on the same base: the burden is identical for a minimum-wage worker and a millionaire buying the same groceries.

**Utility taxes.** Regressive for the same reason as sales tax: utilities are a fixed cost that represents a larger share of lower incomes.

**Flat parcel taxes.** A fixed dollar amount per parcel. A $200/year parcel tax is negligible to a $2M homeowner and meaningful to a renter in a $400K condo (passed through in rent).

**Impact fees (on future residents).** Not a tax on current residents, but a tax on construction that functions as a regressive cost on future housing supply. The people who ultimately pay are future renters and buyers in higher prices and reduced supply. See Impact Fees section below.

### Progressive (burden scales with ability to pay)

**Income tax.** California's is the most progressive in the nation. Top marginal rate 13.3%. Directly scales with ability to pay.

**Land value tax.** Progressive because land ownership is concentrated among the wealthy. A pure LVT cannot be passed to tenants because land supply is fixed; the tax is absorbed by the landowner, not shifted. See [land-value-tax](land-value-tax.md).

**High-threshold transfer taxes.** Progressive when structured with high thresholds (like Measure ULA's $5M+ trigger). Flat-rate transfer taxes are mildly progressive since transaction values correlate with wealth.

### Mixed / depends on structure

**Property tax (traditional).** Theoretically progressive (wealth correlates with property value). In California, Prop 13 makes it deeply regressive in practice: longtime owners pay assessments frozen near purchase price while new buyers and renters (via pass-through) pay market rates. See Prop 13 section below.

**Special assessments.** Depends on structure. If based on property frontage/area, roughly proportional. If flat per-parcel, regressive.

**TIF (Tax Increment Financing).** Not a tax itself; it redirects property tax growth in a defined area. Effect depends entirely on what the increment funds. See TIF section below.

## Impact Fees: The Hidden Tax on Housing Supply

Impact fees are California YIMBY's central tax fight, and the data supports the priority.

### The numbers

- **California average multifamily:** $21,703/unit (national average: $8,034, per CA YIMBY/UCLA 2024 report)
- **California average single-family:** $37,471/unit (national: $13,627)
- **California vs Texas:** $29,000/unit vs under $1,000/unit (RAND)
- **Extremes:** Fremont: $86,000/unit. Some California cities: $150,000/unit total fees

### The production impact

RAND estimates a 25% reduction in California impact fees could "significantly increase housing production." Terner Center found fees are under 5% of total project cost on average, but the marginal impact is larger: fees often determine whether a project pencils at all, particularly for affordable and missing-middle housing.

### Why impact fees are regressive

Impact fees make future residents pay upfront for infrastructure that benefits the entire community. The fee is passed through in higher rents and purchase prices. A $50,000/unit impact fee on a 100-unit project adds $5M to the project cost; that cost is ultimately borne by future tenants paying higher rent.

The alternative (funding infrastructure from general revenue, property tax, or land value tax) spreads the cost across existing residents who also benefit from the infrastructure. Impact fees shift the burden onto newcomers, who are disproportionately younger, lower-income, and renters.

### CA YIMBY's position

CA YIMBY sponsored AB 602 (fee transparency and reform). Their 2024 report with UCLA argues impact fees "can be a tool to stymie growth." A new 2026 California law reportedly reduces fees by up to $70K/unit in some cases.

### The Elmendorf-Shanske alternative

Elmendorf and Shanske (UC Davis) proposed replacing impact fees with a tax on upzoning-created land value: capture the windfall from entitlements rather than taxing the construction. This aligns the incentive (build) with the revenue mechanism (value capture). They note a pure LVT approach is "likely foreclosed by Prop 13" but value capture on rezoning events may be feasible.

## Transfer Taxes: Measure ULA Case Study

Real estate transfer taxes can fund affordable housing but may reduce production. The design determines the balance.

### Measure ULA (Los Angeles, 2022)

- 4% tax on sales above $5M; 5.5% above $10M
- Revenue dedicated to affordable housing and tenant assistance
- UCLA Lewis Center (2025): reduced multifamily production by ~1,910 units/year (18% decline among 20+ unit projects)
- RAND (2026): reportedly found a 46% cut in multifamily investment (secondary source)
- City review of seven studies: split; three found negative impacts, four found no negative impact

### Design lessons

The rate and threshold matter enormously. ULA's 4-5.5% rate is high enough to change transaction behavior. Lower rates or higher thresholds would likely have smaller effects. San Francisco's older 1.5% transfer tax has not shown comparable production impacts.

For housing advocacy: transfer taxes on high-value commercial and luxury transactions can fund affordable housing without significant production loss. Transfer taxes on the range where multifamily projects transact ($5M-$20M) directly hit the housing production pipeline.

## Inclusionary Zoning as a De Facto Tax

Inclusionary requirements function as a tax on market-rate development to subsidize below-market units. Whether this helps or hurts housing depends on the stringency and design.

### Evidence

- UC Irvine (Kouchekinia 2026, working paper): each additional point of inclusionary stringency reduces new permits by ~7.6% using statewide California data
- Earlier studies were split: Schuetz/Meltzer found no significant effect in the Bay Area; Reason Foundation found declines in 13 cities
- Composition shifts: toward multifamily and smaller units

### The YIMBY analytical framework

Inclusionary requirements set citywide = pro-housing (+1). Demanding a specific compliant project exceed existing requirements as grounds for denial = anti-housing (-2). The test: did they cite inclusionary shortfall to DENY a project that met existing rules? That is weaponization, not policy.

## TIF Districts: Design-Dependent

Tax Increment Financing redirects the growth in property tax revenue within a defined area to fund improvements in that area. Whether TIF produces housing depends entirely on policy design.

### Evidence by city

- **Portland** (45% housing set-aside): 5,300 affordable units, 4x housing growth rate in TIF areas
- **Chicago** (no housing set-aside): only 4% of TIF funds went to affordable housing; funded housing was more expensive than existing stock
- **Houston:** zero TIF funds directed to affordable housing

### Scoring rubric

A TIF district is pro-housing if: (1) it has a binding affordable housing set-aside of 30%+, (2) it's located in a transit-served or jobs-rich area, and (3) the housing produced is additional (wouldn't have been built anyway). Without these conditions, TIF tends to subsidize commercial development and capture value for existing landowners.

## Prop 13 and the Fiscalization of Land Use

### The mechanism

Prop 13 (1978) capped property tax at 1% of purchase price with 2%/year assessment increases. This slashed city property tax revenue, making cities dependent on sales tax. Cities therefore zone for auto malls and big-box retail (sales tax generators) over housing (cost centers). This is called "fiscalization of land use" (term coined by Misczynski, 1986).

### The evidence

The direct zoning-for-retail effect is debated. The LAO found "limited evidence" that cities with lower property tax shares built less housing. But Prop 13's broader distortions clearly harm housing production:

- **Discourages improvement.** Improving property triggers reassessment; holding underused land is rewarded with a frozen tax basis.
- **Encourages speculation.** Low carrying costs on land make it profitable to hold vacant or underused parcels indefinitely.
- **Creates generational tax inequality.** A new buyer pays 5-10x the property tax of a neighbor who bought in the 1980s, for identical services. Renters (via pass-through) subsidize long-tenured owners.
- **Blocks LVT.** California's constitution forecloses split-rate property taxation, the instrument economists most agree would increase housing production.
- **Forces regressive revenue tools.** With property tax capped, cities turn to sales taxes, impact fees, and bonds, all of which are more regressive or more distortionary than a properly structured property or land value tax.

### What Prop 13 blocks

- Land value taxation (can't split the rate)
- Progressive property reassessment (locked at 2%/year)
- Meaningful local property tax revenue from new development (capped)

This forces cities toward sales taxes, fees, and bonds for revenue; all worse instruments for housing.

## Federal Tax Expenditures

### LIHTC (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit)

The largest federal housing production tool. Advocates claim it produces 50% of all multifamily housing starts annually. CRS is more skeptical: "evidence is unclear" it helps solve affordable housing, and it "may actually crowd out other affordable housing development." Scale: $54.6B in tax expenditures FY2020-2024.

### Mortgage Interest Deduction (MID)

Captured almost entirely by incumbent homeowners. Hilber and Turner (2014): "fails to promote homeownership." Disproportionately benefits higher-income households. Simulations show repealing it would lower house prices and increase homeownership. Scale: $125.2B in tax expenditures FY2020-2024, 2.3x LIHTC's cost for demonstrably worse housing outcomes.

The MID also reinforces NIMBYism: homeowners whose largest asset's value depends on restricting supply have a direct financial incentive to oppose new housing.

### Opportunity Zones

Contested. EIG (proponent) claims 416,000 additional housing units by Q1 2025. A peer-reviewed study found "no statistically significant effects on LIHTC outcomes." DePaul's Institute for Housing Studies warned OZs "could accelerate gentrification." Displacement evidence is thin either way.

### Capital Gains Exclusion ($250K/$500K on primary residence)

Encourages homeownership but also encourages holding property as an investment vehicle. Contributes to the homeowner-voter dynamic that drives NIMBYism.

## Framework: Evaluating Revenue Measures Through a Housing Lens

When a local revenue measure appears on the ballot, evaluate it on four dimensions:

### 1. Who bears the burden?

Is the tax progressive (income-scaled, wealth-based, land value) or regressive (sales, flat parcel, utility)? Regressive taxes require a higher bar of justification because they disproportionately burden renters and low-income residents, the populations most affected by the housing crisis.

### 2. Does it incentivize or discourage housing production?

- **Incentivizes:** LVT, split-rate property tax, impact fee reductions
- **Neutral:** Income tax, most sales taxes, hotel taxes
- **Discourages:** High impact fees, high-rate transfer taxes on multifamily transaction ranges, inclusionary requirements above ~15%

### 3. Does the jurisdiction have a track record of enabling housing?

Revenue is only pro-housing if the entity uses it to add supply. A city under HCD enforcement action, facing housing element lawsuits, or with a pattern of blocking projects does not get the benefit of the doubt. In that context, more revenue may simply fund the anti-housing apparatus.

Questions to ask:
- Is the city's housing element certified by HCD?
- Has it been sued for housing non-compliance? By whom?
- What is its project approval rate for discretionary housing?
- Has it adopted state density bonus, SB 9, SB 79 in good faith?

### 4. Does it change the development pattern or subsidize the existing one?

A Strong Towns analysis asks: does this revenue maintain a fiscally unsustainable sprawl pattern, or does it invest in compact, productive land use? A sales tax that funds suburban road maintenance perpetuates the Growth Ponzi Scheme. A revenue source that funds transit, infill infrastructure, or downtown redevelopment can break the cycle.

### Scoring summary

A revenue measure is **pro-housing** when it: uses a progressive tax instrument, funds infrastructure that enables housing production, goes to a jurisdiction with a housing-enabling track record, and invests in fiscally productive land use patterns.

A revenue measure is **anti-housing** (or at best neutral) when it: uses a regressive instrument, funds general operations in a jurisdiction that blocks housing, or maintains sprawl infrastructure without changing the development pattern.
