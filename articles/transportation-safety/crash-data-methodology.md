# Transportation Safety — Crash Data Methodology & Evidence Base

## Overview

Transportation safety is an empirical question with a large peer-reviewed evidence base. When someone claims protected bike infrastructure is dangerous, apply the same scrutiny you'd apply to any claim that contradicts the scientific consensus: check the methodology, check the credentials, check consistency with the global evidence base, and check who benefits from the conclusion.

---

## Evidence Hierarchy

1. **Peer-reviewed meta-analyses and systematic reviews**
   - Teschke et al. 2012 (*BMJ*): 14 route types; cycle tracks = 9x safer than major streets with parked cars
   - Marshall & Ferenchak 2019 (*J Transport & Health*): 12 US cities, 13 years — 44% fewer fatalities, 50% fewer serious injuries with protected infrastructure
   - Reynolds et al. 2009 (*Environ Health Perspect*): first systematic review of facility-type risk

2. **Peer-reviewed empirical studies with large samples**
   - Cicchino et al. 2020 (*J Safety Research*): IIHS, 12 cities — 44% lower odds of injury in protected lanes
   - Lusk et al. 2011 (*Injury Prevention*): Montreal, 6 years — 28% lower injury rate on cycle tracks
   - Harris et al. 2013 (*Am J Public Health*): Vancouver, 14 route types — cycle tracks = lowest injury risk
   - DiGioia et al. 2017 (*Accident Analysis & Prevention*): meta-analysis of CMFs for bike facilities

3. **Government research with expert review**
   - FHWA-HRT-23-078 (Dixon et al. 2023): CMF of 0.47 for flex-post SBLs (53% crash reduction, mid-block only)
   - NACTO design guidance
   - AASHTO *Guide for the Development of Bicycle Facilities*

4. **Municipal crash data analyses with transparent methodology**

5. **Self-published reports, advocacy papers, council presentations** — claims requiring verification, not evidence

---

## What the Evidence Shows

- **Protected bike lanes reduce injuries and fatalities.** Every meta-analysis finds this. Magnitude varies; direction does not.
- **No protected bike lane in peer-reviewed literature has produced a net increase in crashes.**
- **Safety compounds with network effects.** Connected networks outperform isolated segments.
- **Design matters enormously.** Flex posts ≠ curb-separated ≠ parking-protected ≠ grade-separated.
- **The comparison baseline is mixing with car traffic, which kills people.**

### Legitimate Design Concerns

- **Intersection right hooks** — real design problem, not an argument against the category
- **Narrow lanes** (< 7 ft) — speed differential hazards with e-bikes
- **Fixed-object collisions** — bollards/wheel stops create hazards; minimize while maintaining separation
- **Maintenance** — debris accumulation forces cyclists into traffic

These are arguments for **better design**, not removal.

## Crash Data Methodology Checklist

### Denominator / Exposure
- Reports crash RATES (per trip, per mile, per rider) or raw COUNTS?
- Counts without denominators are meaningless for safety assessment
- More riders = more total crashes even if per-rider risk drops
- No ridership data = no safety conclusion possible

### Statistical Testing
- Test of significance? (chi-square, Fisher's exact, Poisson regression)
- Confidence intervals reported?
- Sample size? (N < 50 crashes = underpowered)
- Multiple testing corrected?

### Study Design
- Before-after (longitudinal): preferred
- Cross-sectional: weaker, more confounders
- Case report (N=1 corridor): hypothesis-generating only
- Ecological (city-level): association only

### Data Source Integrity
- Sources identified? (SWITRS/TIMS, CCRS, police reports, EMS)
- Sources consistent between comparison periods?
- Crowdsourced data mixed with official data?
- Police report under-capture acknowledged? (40-92% of bicycle crashes missing)

### Confounders Controlled
- Ridership changes, e-bike adoption, COVID, seasonal patterns
- Vehicle speeds and volumes
- Road geometry changes
- Demographics of road users
- Weather, lighting, construction

### Crash Type Accounting
- ALL crash types or subset?
- Vehicle-bicycle, solo bicycle, bicycle-pedestrian?
- Intersection AND mid-block?
- Fixed-object crashes compared to vehicle-caused crashes?

### Control Comparison
- Control corridor/period exists?
- Control genuinely comparable?

## Author Credential Check

Legitimate credentials:
- Faculty in transportation engineering, public health, or urban planning
- Publication record in: *Accident Analysis & Prevention*, *Transportation Research Record*, *J Safety Research*, *J Transport & Health*, *Injury Prevention*
- PE license in transportation/traffic engineering
- Research affiliation: IIHS, SafeTREC, VTTI, HSRC

Red flags:
- PhD in unrelated field presented as transportation expertise
- Zero peer-reviewed publications in transportation safety
- League of American Bicyclists Certified Instructor (advocacy cert, not research)
- "Former traffic commissioner" (volunteer role)

## Manipulation Patterns

### Methodological Laundering
Mix unverified data (Facebook groups, self-reports) with official data (SWITRS/CCRS), present through government channels as sound methodology, export to other jurisdictions as precedent.

**Break it:** Demand data source separation. Which crashes are CCRS? Which are crowdsourced?

### Counts Without Rates
Report raw crash counts increasing. Ignore ridership changes. A corridor going from 500 to 2,000 daily riders with crashes from 10 to 15 got SAFER (rate: 2.0% → 0.75%).

**Break it:** Demand the denominator.

### Selective Crash Typing
Count only infrastructure-caused crashes (hitting bollards) while ignoring prevented crashes (rear-end, sideswipe from mixing with traffic).

**Break it:** Demand full crash-type accounting.

### N=1 Generalization
Study one corridor, find an increase, generalize to "separated bikeways are dangerous." One adverse reaction doesn't invalidate a Phase III trial.

**Break it:** Point to the meta-analyses.

### Vehicular Cycling Ideology
Start from the position that cyclists should mix with traffic (John Forester, 1970s). Collect supporting data. Recommend removal of separation. Never disclose the ideological commitment.

**Identifiers:** Replacing Class IV with Class II, "experienced cyclists" language, CyclingSavvy affiliation, emphasis on solo crashes, dismissal of "perceived safety."

**Break it:** Vehicular cycling is discredited theory. The data shows the opposite of its predictions.

### The Regional Export
Produce questionable analysis in one city, present at neighboring councils to justify identical policy across the region.

**Break it:** Each city must evaluate independently. Is the presenter credentialed? Was the data peer-reviewed?

## California Crash Data Sources

- **SWITRS/TIMS/CCRS**: UC Berkeley SafeTREC. Geocoded, verified, severity-coded. Gold standard.
- **CCRS on CA Open Data Portal**: `data.ca.gov/dataset/ccrs`. Three CSVs per year: Crashes, Parties, InjuredWitnessPassengers. Linked by CollisionId.
- **EMS dispatch logs**: Available via PRA from local fire departments. Systematic, legitimate supplementary source.
- **Strava Metro**: Sample bias toward recreational riders. Not representative.

## Rebuttal Templates

**"Protected bike lanes cause more crashes"** — The global evidence base consistently shows protected infrastructure reduces injuries and fatalities. An N=1 case report from authors with no transportation safety publications cannot overturn this.

**"Cyclists are safer mixing with traffic"** — This is vehicular cycling theory (Forester, 1970s). Tested and refuted by 40+ years of data. Teschke: cycle tracks 9x safer. Marshall & Ferenchak: 44% fewer fatalities.

**"We should wait for better data"** — The default condition kills people now. The question is not "is the evidence perfect?" but "is protected infrastructure better than the status quo?" The evidence overwhelmingly says yes.

**"Crashes with bollards prove the infrastructure is dangerous"** — One-sided accounting. Compare bollard crashes to the car-bicycle crashes prevented. If 5 bollard crashes replaced 20 car strikes, the infrastructure saved 15 people from worse outcomes.

**"This data shows a 400% increase"** — Check: count or rate? Denominator? Same data sources both periods? Statistical test? Who compiled it? Peer-reviewed? If any fail, the number is not evidence.
