# Automated Traffic Enforcement — Evidence Base

## Overview

Automated traffic enforcement (ATE) uses cameras to detect and cite red light running (RLR) and speeding violations. The peer-reviewed evidence base spans 30+ years, 20+ countries, and hundreds of studies. The consensus: cameras reduce serious injuries and fatalities at signalized intersections. The only meta-analysis that found otherwise (Erke 2009) was directly rebutted for methodological errors and superseded by updated analysis from the same institution.

This article covers red light cameras (RLCs), fixed speed cameras, and section control (average speed enforcement). All three share the same evidence pattern: violations drop, serious crashes drop, rear-end crashes may increase modestly, and net safety benefit is positive.

## Evidence Hierarchy for ATE

### Tier 1: Meta-analyses and systematic reviews

| Study | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| Hoye 2013 (*Acc Anal Prev*) | RLCs reduce injury crashes 13%. Updated meta-analysis superseding Erke 2009. |
| Cohn et al. 2020 (Campbell Collaboration) | 38 studies. RLCs reduce violations, injury crashes, right-angle crashes. |
| Aeron-Thomas & Hess 2005 (Cochrane) | Cameras cut injury crashes ~30%. |
| Shaaban & Pande 2019 (*Acc Anal Prev*) | 2013-2017 studies confirm angle and left-turn crash reductions. |
| Wilson et al. 2010 (Cochrane) | Speed cameras: collisions down 5-69%, injuries 12-65%, deaths 17-71%. |
| Pilkington & Kinra 2005 (*BMJ*) | Speed camera systematic review. Consistent casualty reductions. |
| Hoye 2014 (*Acc Anal Prev*) | Section control reduces KSI crashes 56%. Greater effect than fixed cameras. |
| Fitzsimmons et al. 2014 (*Traffic Inj Prev*) | All 9 US studies showed significant reductions in violations, crashes, and injuries. |

### Tier 2: Large-scale empirical studies

| Study | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| Hu & Cicchino 2017 (IIHS, *J Safety Res*) | 57 cities. Cameras on: 21% fewer fatal RLR crashes. Cameras off: 30% more. |
| Council et al. 2005 (FHWA-HRT-05-048) | 132 sites, 7 jurisdictions. Right-angle crashes down 25%, injuries down 16%. Net economic benefit. |
| Hu et al. 2020 (IIHS) | Montgomery County MD: 39% reduction in incapacitating/fatal injury likelihood. |
| Texas statewide evaluation (254 intersections) | RLR crashes down 20%, right-angle RLR crashes down 24%. |
| Tilahun 2023 (*TRR*) | Chicago speed cameras: fatal/injury crashes down 12%, severe injuries down 15%. |
| Guerra et al. 2024 (*TRR*) | Philadelphia Roosevelt Blvd speed cameras: ~520 injury collisions prevented in 2 years. |
| Rwanda 2024 (*Acc Anal Prev*) | 38% monthly decrease in death incidence after camera installation. |

### Tier 3: City/state evaluations

Retting & Kyrychenko 2002 (Oxnard CA), Ko et al. 2013 (Charlotte NC), Houston 2017, Llau et al. 2015 (Miami-Dade), Wong 2014 (Los Angeles), McCartt & Hu 2014 (Arlington VA), Shin & Washington 2007 (Arizona), Sayed & de Leur 2007 (Edmonton), Chen et al. 2014 (Winnipeg), Golob et al. 2002 (San Diego), Bhandari et al. 2025 (most recent US study).

### Tier 4: Government reports and recommendations

CDC MV PICCS recommends RLCs. DOJ CrimeSolutions rates them "Promising." CRS R46552 (non-partisan congressional report). NHTSA operational guidelines. UK College of Policing: deaths and serious injuries down 42%. WHO Speed Management Manual recommends automated enforcement.

## The Rear-End Tradeoff

The most common objection: "cameras increase rear-end crashes." This is real but misleading.

**What the data shows:**
- RLCs reduce right-angle crashes (the deadly ones) by 20-47%
- RLCs increase rear-end crashes by 15-40%
- Right-angle crashes are 3-5x more likely to cause serious injury or death than rear-end crashes
- Net safety benefit is consistently positive in studies that measure severity, not just counts

**FHWA-HRT-05-048 conclusion (often misquoted):** "There was indeed a modest aggregate crash cost benefit of RLC systems." The study explicitly evaluated whether rear-end increases negate right-angle decreases. Answer: they do not.

**The tradeoff is disappearing:** IIHS 2025 found that automatic emergency braking (AEB) eliminates the rear-end crash increase associated with RLCs. As AEB fleet penetration grows, the tradeoff shrinks to zero.

## What Happens When Cameras Are Removed

This is the strongest natural experiment in the literature:

- **Hu & Cicchino 2017:** 14 cities that turned off cameras saw fatal RLR crash rates 30% higher than expected.
- **Houston 2017:** RLR crashes up 20%, right-angle crashes up 23% after deactivation. Adverse spillover at nearby non-camera intersections.
- **Ko et al. 2013 (Charlotte):** Crashes increased after cameras removed in 2006.
- **NYC DOT 2024:** RLR down 73%, T-bone crashes down 65%, rear-end crashes down 49% at camera sites. Expanding from 150 to 600 intersections.

If cameras don't work, removing them should have no effect. It doesn't. Crashes go up every time.

## Section Control (Average Speed Enforcement)

Point-to-point cameras measure average speed over a distance, not instantaneous speed at a point. Consistently outperform fixed cameras:

- Montella et al. 2012: 31.2% total crash reduction on Italian motorway
- De Pauw et al. 2014: KSI reduced 49% in Norway
- Hoye 2014 meta-analysis: KSI crashes down 56% (vs ~20% for fixed cameras)
- Agent-based simulation (2024): P2P cameras reduce crash risk 3-11%, outperform fixed in all scenarios

## Public Support

Public opposition is louder than actual sentiment:

- McCartt & Hu 2012: Two thirds of drivers in 14 camera cities favor enforcement. 42% strongly favor.
- DC survey (2014): Most residents support speed cameras and RLCs.
- AAA Foundation 2024: 80% of drivers say running reds is very/extremely dangerous.
- Ralph et al. 2024: Reform-focused framing (safety, not revenue) increases public acceptance.

## Common Opposition Arguments and Rebuttals

### "Cameras are just revenue generators"
Revenue and safety are not mutually exclusive. Seatbelt laws generate fine revenue too. The question is whether violations decrease (they do, 40-50%) and whether crashes decrease (they do). Programs with declining ticket revenue over time are succeeding, not failing.

### "Just fix the signal timing"
Longer yellow timing reduces violations ~36% (Retting et al. 2008). Adding cameras reduces violations another 96% on top of that. They're complementary, not alternatives. Also: signal timing doesn't help when drivers run lights 2+ seconds after red, which is when fatal crashes happen.

### "The one meta-analysis found cameras don't work"
Erke 2009 found a non-significant 15% increase in total crashes. Burkey & Obeng 2009 published a direct rebuttal in the same journal, identifying methodological errors. Hoye 2013 (from the same institution as Erke) then published an updated meta-analysis finding cameras reduce injury crashes 13%. The scientific record corrected itself.

### "Our city is different"
No city in the peer-reviewed literature has found that cameras increase fatal crashes. The effect is consistent across Oxnard CA, Houston TX, Charlotte NC, Arlington VA, San Diego CA, New York NY, Edmonton, Winnipeg, Singapore, Sydney, Melbourne, Saudi Arabia, Rwanda, Bogota, and dozens more. The burden of proof is on the person claiming their city is the exception.

### "Site selection matters more"
Yes, and that's an argument FOR studying cameras, not against deploying them. Council votes to study camera placement are exactly the process that produces good site selection. Opposing the study while claiming to support proper methodology is incoherent.

## California Law: SB 720

SB 720 (Sen. Ashby, signed 2025) expands red light camera authorization in California. Previously limited to a handful of pilot cities; now available statewide. Key provisions:
- Cities must conduct engineering study before deployment
- Revenue must fund traffic safety programs
- Yellow timing must meet or exceed ITE standards
- Data privacy protections for captured images
