# Evaluate Bike Lane Business Closure Claim

## Prerequisites

- The specific business name and address
- The street where the bike lane was installed
- The news article or social media post making the claim

## Steps

### Step 1: Verify the Parking Situation

Determine whether the business relies on street parking or has off-street parking.

1. Search for the address on commercial real estate sites (LoopNet, Duhs Commercial, CoStar) to find leasing brochures with site plans
2. Check Google Maps satellite view for off-street parking lots
3. Read Yelp/TripAdvisor reviews mentioning parking — customers describe the actual experience
4. Search for the shopping center or plaza name + "parking"

**Key question**: Does the business have its own parking lot? If yes, street parking removal from bike lanes is unlikely to be the primary access point for customers.

### Step 2: Check Citywide Business Closure Rates

A single closure means nothing without baseline context.

1. Search: "[city name] restaurant closures [year] rising costs statistics"
2. Check local business journalism (SanDiegoVille, Eater, local alt-weeklies)
3. Find California Restaurant Association or National Restaurant Association survey data for the metro area
4. Compare: Are businesses closing at similar rates on streets WITHOUT bike lanes?

**Key question**: Is this closure part of a broader trend (cost crisis, recession, pandemic aftermath) or genuinely isolated to the bike lane corridor?

### Step 3: Investigate the Business's Financial History

Look for signs of financial stress unrelated to bike lanes.

1. Check California Secretary of State business filings (bizfileonline.sos.ca.gov) for entity status
2. Search for recent expansion (second locations, renovations, outside financing) — overexpansion during downturns is a common cause of failure
3. Check BBB profile for incorporation date and any complaints
4. Search for investment or loan announcements (Mission Driven Finance, SBA, CDFI lenders)
5. Look at the timeline: Did the business invest in renovations shortly before closing? (Inconsistent with a gradual parking-driven decline)

**Key question**: Was the business financially stretched by expansion, renovation, or rising costs before the bike lane was installed?

### Step 4: Examine What the Owner Actually Said

Read the full article, not just the headline.

1. List ALL reasons the owner gave for closing (costs, traffic, rent, parking, bike lanes)
2. Note exact quotes — did the owner say bike lanes were THE cause or PARTIALLY to blame?
3. Check if the owner said "nobody even did a study" or similar admissions
4. Note whether the owner has another location still operating

**Key question**: Did the news outlet headline the bike lane angle while the owner cited multiple factors?

### Step 5: Evaluate the News Coverage

Check whether the outlet did any independent verification.

1. Did they interview anyone besides the owner and immediate neighbors?
2. Did they contact the city transportation department for comment?
3. Did they cite any data (sales figures, parking utilization, customer counts)?
4. Did they mention the citywide closure trend?
5. Did they reference any research on bike lanes and business impact?
6. Did they note whether the business has off-street parking?

**Key question**: Is this journalism (verified, contextualized) or an amplified anecdote?

### Step 6: Cross-Reference the Evidence Base

Compare the claim against peer-reviewed findings. See [bike-lane-economic-impact](articles/active-transport/bike-lane-economic-impact.md).

Key points to cite:
- No peer-reviewed study has found bike lanes cause catastrophic business harm
- Merchants systematically overestimate car-customer share (Toronto: <10% by car; Vancouver: 20% actual vs. 40% estimated)
- NYC 9th Avenue: +49% retail sales after protected bike lane
- Caltrans: No significant sales change from Class II bike lanes statewide

### Step 7: Write the Assessment

Structure as:
1. **The claim** — what was alleged, by whom, in what outlet
2. **Parking reality** — off-street lot? Street parking dependency?
3. **Citywide context** — baseline closure rates
4. **Financial history** — expansion, renovation, cost pressures
5. **Media quality** — what was verified vs. amplified
6. **Evidence base** — what peer-reviewed research shows
7. **Probable cause** — what likely drove the closure

## Constraints

| Constraint | Rationale |
|---|---|
| Never claim bike lanes definitely helped THIS business | The evidence shows population-level trends, not individual causation |
| Acknowledge genuine evidence gaps (suburban corridors, construction period) | Intellectual honesty strengthens the argument |
| Distinguish street parking from off-street parking | The entire claim often rests on conflating these |
| Check for construction-period disruption separately | Temporary construction impacts are real even if permanent impacts are positive |

## Outputs

- Fact-check assessment (structured per Step 7)
- Reusable for op-eds, public comment, social media responses
- Cite specific studies with DOIs for credibility
